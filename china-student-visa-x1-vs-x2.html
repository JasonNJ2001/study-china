China Student Visa X1 vs X2: Complete Comparison

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-10-21

Most applicants to Chinese universities spend weeks comparing rankings and days on the visa. It should be the other way round. The visa class printed in your passport decides how long you may legally stay, whether you can leave and re-enter without a fresh application, and whether the bank, the telecom counter and the dorm office treat you as a resident or as a visitor. X1 versus X2 looks like paperwork. It is the single decision that shapes your first year.

The only variable that matters: 180 days

Chinese student visas are split by duration, not by degree level, university ranking or scholarship status.

  • X1 is issued for study programmes lasting more than 180 days.
  • X2 is issued for study programmes of 180 days or less.

That is the complete rule. A one-year Chinese language course, a four-year bachelor's degree, a two-semester master's — all X1. A six-week summer school, a one-month intensive programme, a single exchange semester that ends inside six months — X2. Neither you nor the visa officer chooses this. The length written on your admission letter and your JW202 form decides it. Every X1 vs X2 visa China question reduces to the same sentence, and most of the expensive mistakes come from answering it by intuition instead of by counting weeks.

Two rows in that table cause almost every problem we see.

Misjudgment 1: "Duration of stay 000 means I cannot stay"

This is the most common panic, and it is a misreading. An X1 visa is routinely printed with a duration of stay of 000, and students assume the consulate issued a broken visa.

000 means the visa itself grants no stay period. The stay period comes later, from the residence permit. You enter on the X1, and within 30 days of arrival you apply at the local exit-entry administration for a residence permit for study, which is a sticker in your passport valid for the length of your programme. The 000 is not a defect. It is the design.

The consequence is what matters: an X1 gives you roughly one month of legal cover, not four years. If you land on 1 September and do nothing, you are in overstay territory by early October.

Misjudgment 2: "I will extend the X2 once I am there"

Students choose X2 for a five-month programme believing it can be topped up in China if they decide to stay for a degree. In practice, X2 is not built to be extended. It has no route into a residence permit, and exit-entry administrations treat extension requests case by case rather than as an entitlement. Building a multi-year plan on an in-country conversion is how people end up flying home in month six to re-apply.

The clean remedy is the boring one: if there is any realistic chance you will continue studying, ask your university for a programme longer than 180 days and the matching JW202, and apply for X1 from the start.

Misjudgment 3: reading the visa expiry as your study end date

An X1 visa carries a validity window — the dates between which you may enter China. That window is typically measured in weeks or a few months, and it has nothing to do with when your course finishes. We regularly meet students who booked a late-September flight against a visa that expired in August, then treated the resulting refusal at check-in as a university problem.

Read three dates separately before you book anything:

  1. The visa's expiry date — your deadline to enter China.
  2. The programme start date on your admission letter.
  3. The 30-day residence permit window, which starts on your date of entry, not on enrolment day.

Misjudgment 4: assuming the documents match

Your admission letter, your JW202 form and your visa application must agree on your name spelling, passport number, university name and programme length. A single mismatch — a middle name dropped on the JW202, a programme listed as 24 weeks on the letter but 30 weeks on the form — triggers a request for new documents, and a request for new documents from abroad costs weeks. Check all three side by side before you pay the visa fee, not after.

If you already hold the wrong visa

This happens, and it is fixable, but the fix depends entirely on where you are standing when you notice.

Still abroad, visa not yet used

Contact the university's international office for a corrected admission letter or JW202, then re-apply. Do not travel first and hope to sort it out on campus — a consulate abroad can issue a new visa in days, an exit-entry office inside China cannot issue an X1 to someone who entered on X2.

Already in China on X2, programme ends inside 180 days

Finish the X2, leave before the duration of stay expires, and apply for X1 from outside mainland China for the longer programme. Confirm the specific consulate's requirements first: rules differ by post, and some are considerably stricter for applicants who are not legally resident in that country.

Already in China on X2 and overstaying

Go to the exit-entry administration voluntarily and immediately. Overstay penalties scale with the number of days overstayed, and a voluntary report made before enforcement is treated very differently from being found.

On X1 approaching day 30 with no permit yet

This is the emergency case, and it is the one worth interrupting your week for. Take your passport, admission letter, JW202, medical certificate, accommodation registration and photographs to the exit-entry office the same week. Most universities have a staff member who walks students through exactly this, and asking early costs nothing.

A short pre-application checklist

  • Programme length in weeks, taken from the admission letter rather than from the brochure.
  • JW202 or JW201 in hand, with name and passport number matching your passport exactly.
  • Visa class chosen to match that length: over 180 days means X1.
  • Accommodation address ready, because you must register with the local police within 24 hours of arrival.
  • Medical examination arranged, since the residence permit usually requires one.
  • A calendar reminder set for entry date plus 20 days, labelled "residence permit".

None of this is difficult. It is simply unforgiving of assumptions, because the two visas look interchangeable on paper and behave nothing alike once you are inside the country. Get the 180-day question right at the application stage, and the rest of the year runs on rails.
