Bicycles and E-Bikes for International Students in China: Buying, Rules and Theft

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-17

A bicycle is the cheapest way to make a Chinese campus and its surrounding city feel small, but the vehicle you buy decides which rules apply to you. A pedal bicycle needs nothing but a lock; an electric two-wheeler can require a plate, a helmet and, in some cities, a licence you probably cannot obtain on a student visa.

Bicycles and E-Bikes Sit on Different Sides of the Law

The dividing line is set by the national standard GB 17761-2018, which defines a compliant electric bicycle as one limited to 25 km/h, weighing no more than 55 kg and fitted with functional pedals. Anything faster or heavier is classed as an electric motorcycle, which is a motor vehicle requiring a Chinese motorcycle licence, insurance and a registration plate.

Why this matters before you pay

Sellers in markets and on second-hand platforms routinely offer "upgraded" models that exceed 25 km/h. Buying one makes you the rider of an unregistered motor vehicle, and a police stop in a city with strict enforcement can end in a fine, confiscation, or a demand for a licence you do not hold. A foreign driving licence is not accepted as a substitute for a Chinese motorcycle licence.

The pedal bicycle

An ordinary bicycle needs no registration, no plate, no insurance and no licence anywhere in China. For a student living within three kilometres of campus, it remains the lowest-risk option by a wide margin, and second-hand bicycles sell for ¥100–300 in June when graduating students leave.

Registering an E-Bike

Compliant e-bikes must be registered with the local traffic police and fitted with a plate, usually at a service point near the district vehicle office. You need the purchase invoice, the certificate of conformity (合格证), your passport and a residence permit. Registration is normally free and takes an afternoon, and the plate is tied to the vehicle rather than to you.

Campus registration is separate

Most universities run their own vehicle registration through the security office (保卫处), issuing a coloured sticker or campus plate. An e-bike with a city plate but no campus sticker is often refused entry at the gate, and unregistered vehicles parked inside the walls are removed to a holding area and released only after a fine.

Campus Rules Are Stricter Than City Rules

Several universities have banned student e-bikes outright since 2021, citing battery fires, and others restrict them to staff or to students with a documented mobility need. The rules change with each intake, so check the current student handbook rather than the advice of a second-year student who bought one before the ban.

The charging rule that catches everyone

Since the Ministry of Emergency Management regulation on high-rise fire safety took effect on 1 August 2021, charging e-bike batteries in lifts, corridors or dormitory rooms is prohibited, and universities enforce it by inspection. Charging points are provided at designated sheds, usually paid per hour through an app. Students caught charging in a dormitory face disciplinary action and, at some universities, confiscation of the vehicle.

Helmets

Helmet requirements are set at provincial level. Jiangsu's e-bike regulation, in force since 1 July 2020, requires riders and passengers to wear helmets, and Zhejiang introduced a similar rule the same month. Enforcement is periodic rather than constant, but a checkpoint near a university gate on a weekday morning is a common sight, and fines are typically ¥20–50.

Theft, Locks and Where to Park

Bicycle theft is common in Chinese cities and seasonal: the risk peaks in September when a new intake buys bikes and again in June when they leave. A ¥30 cable lock is the single most common false economy. Buy a U-lock of hardened steel, lock the frame and the rear wheel to a fixed rack, and use the supervised parking areas that most campuses provide near dormitories.

The routine that works costs about ¥80 and takes one afternoon:

  • Buy a hardened U-lock, not a cable, and lock the frame plus one wheel.
  • Use a fixed rack inside a supervised area rather than a wall or railing.
  • Record the frame number and photograph it before the first ride.
  • Park in the same bay daily, since unfamiliar bikes in an unfamiliar bay attract attention.
  • Never leave lights, helmets or phone mounts attached overnight.

Register the bike with security

Even a bicycle can be registered voluntarily with the campus security office, which records the frame number and issues a sticker. Registration does not prevent theft, but it is what makes recovery possible: recovered bicycles are held by security, and unclaimed unregistered bikes are auctioned or scrapped after a set period.

Where not to park

Fire lanes, building entrances and wheelchair ramps are cleared aggressively, and bicycles parked there are moved or removed within hours. Dormitory notice boards usually name the holding location and the fine, which is typically ¥20–50 plus a lecture from the duty officer.

Shared Bikes as the Default First Month

For the first month, before you know your timetable and your route, shared bikes are the sensible choice for an international student. Meituan, HelloBike and Didi's Qingju cover most Chinese cities, cost about ¥1.5 per 15 minutes, and student monthly passes run ¥10–15. They require a Chinese phone number and either Alipay or WeChat Pay with real-name verification.

What shared bikes do not solve

They cannot be taken into a campus that bans them at the gate, they disappear from residential areas at rush hour, and they are not reliable for a 7 am exam. Students who commute more than four kilometres each way usually end up buying a bicycle within a semester, and the sensible sequence is: shared bikes for the first month, then a used bicycle once you know the route, then an e-bike only if the campus permits it and the commute justifies the paperwork.
