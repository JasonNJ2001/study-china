The Tier System in Chinese Higher Education: Does 一本 or 二本 Matter for International Students

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-13

一本 and 二本 are admission batches for Chinese school leavers sitting the gaokao, and international students are admitted through a separate channel that never touches them. The labels survive in hiring habits and family conversation, but they do not describe the quality of the programme you would join, and they are not a ranking. Here is what the tiers mean and where they still cost or save you money.

What 一本 and 二本 Actually Mean

The two terms describe the batch in which a university recruited gaokao candidates in a given province: 一本 was the first and most selective batch, 二本 the second. They were province-level administrative categories rather than national quality grades, so the same university could sit in a different batch in a neighbouring province and in a different year.

The batches are being abolished

Since the State Council announced the examination and enrolment reform in 2014, province after province has merged the batches. Zhejiang and Shanghai led in 2017, Shandong and Beijing followed, and by the 2025 intake the split survives in only a handful of provinces. A university's 一本 label is therefore a historical description rather than a current admissions fact.

985, 211 and 双一流

Three other labels get confused with the batches. Project 211, launched in 1995, funded 116 universities; Project 985, launched in 1998, funded 39. Both were closed to new entrants and replaced in 2017 by the Double First-Class list (双一流), which in its second round, published in February 2022, named 147 institutions.

Why It Barely Affects International Admission

International applicants are assessed by the university's own international student office on transcripts, language scores, a study plan and sometimes an interview, and the gaokao batch plays no part. A 二本 institution with a strong English-taught engineering programme can admit you on exactly the same terms as a 985, and your admission letter carries no batch label at all.

What does affect your file is whether the programme is taught in English for the whole degree, whether the specific department has facilities you would use, and whether the international office answers email. Those three things predict your experience far better than a batch that was abolished in most provinces before you applied.

Where the Labels Still Matter

The labels matter after graduation more than before it. Chinese employers' HR systems often filter by 985, 211 or 双一流 because those lists are easy to screen against, provincial scholarship panels weight institutional prestige, and research funding per supervisor correlates with tier even though the correlation is far from perfect.

What HR filters actually screen

Recruiters in Beijing and Shanghai typically paste a list — 985, 211, 双一流 — into an applicant tracking system and filter on the university name, so a degree from a respected provincial university can be dropped before a human sees the CV. Employers in Shenzhen and in foreign-invested firms screen far less on labels and much more on internships and English level.

Reading Rankings Instead of Batches

Rankings answer a different question from batches, and they answer it in a currency foreign employers understand. Five mainland universities sit inside the QS top 100, but a subject-level table is more useful than the overall one: a university outside the global top 500 can hold a top-50 position in petroleum engineering, textiles or traditional Chinese medicine.

What to check instead of the tier

Four things predict your experience better than a batch label: whether the programme is taught in English for all four years, whether the department has a laboratory or studio you would actually use, how many international students sit in the cohort, and where graduates of that specific programme work. Ask the international office for the last two years of graduate destinations in writing.

How to Use the Labels Without Being Misled

Treat 985 and 双一流 as signals of research funding and domestic hiring recognition, treat 一本 and 二本 as obsolete, and treat any agent who quotes batch labels as a proxy for teaching quality as uninformed. If a Chinese employer is your target after graduation, ask which list their HR filter screens against before you choose between two offers.

The China university tier system is a domestic admissions artefact that leaks into hiring. It is not a quality measure, and no international student is admitted or rejected on the basis of it. What decides your outcome is the programme, the department and your own Chinese level.

  1. Ask whether the degree is taught in English for the full duration, not just year one.
  2. Ask how many international students graduated from that department last year.
  3. Ask which companies recruited from the programme in the past two intakes.
  4. Ask whether the department's laboratories are open to master's students or reserved for doctoral researchers.
  5. Ask for the graduate destination report, and read it rather than the brochure.

Scholarships and the Prestige Premium

Provincial scholarships often rank applicants partly on institutional prestige, and a 双一流 nomination can carry a larger stipend than the same applicant at a provincial university. That premium is real but narrow: it applies to nomination decisions, not to tuition, and self-funded applicants pay the programme fee regardless of which list the university appears on.

Tuition is set by programme and city rather than by tier. An English-taught business master's at a mid-ranked provincial university can cost more than a Chinese-taught engineering master's at a 985, because the price reflects the language of instruction and the cost of delivery, not the label on the gate.
