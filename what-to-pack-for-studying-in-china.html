"What to Pack for Studying in China: A Month-by-Month Climate Guide"

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-09

Packing for a single climate is the most common expensive mistake students make about China, a country that spans tropical coast, desert basin and Siberian-border winter. A student heading to Harbin and one heading to Guangzhou need almost nothing in common. This guide sorts the list by climate zone, heating rules and local prices.

What should you pack for studying in China?

Pack for your specific city's January and July, not for "China": one winter coat rated for the local minimum, one warm layer for unheated indoor space, electronics with dual-voltage chargers, a two-week supply of prescription medicine with the prescription, and all admission documents in hand luggage. Everything else, from bedding to a rice cooker, costs less in China than the airline charges to carry it.

The two variables are latitude and heating. Municipal central heating stops at the Huai River line, so northern cities are warm indoors in winter and southern cities are colder inside than outside; get those facts right and the rest is arithmetic.

Which climate zone is your university in?

Chinese cities fall into four practical packing zones, and the table gives the numbers that matter: the coldest January night, the hottest July day, and whether the building will be heated in winter. Read your city's row first, because it decides whether you need a down coat or a dehumidifier.

The Yangtze zone is the difficult one, because 0-5C outside with high humidity and no heating produces an indoor chill that a Harbin student never experiences. Kunming is the exception in the south: 1,890 metres of altitude keeps it near 15-20C all year, which is why it is called the Spring City.

Packing for the north: coats, boots and overheated rooms

A north China winter needs one serious coat, rated to minus 20C for the northeast and minus 10C for Beijing, plus thermal base layers, gloves, a hat that covers the ears and boots with grip for ice. Down jackets are cheap and good in China, so if luggage is tight, buy the coat in your first week at a Uniqlo or Decathlon.

The surprise is indoor heat. Municipal heating runs from 15 November and keeps rooms at 18-22C, so students wear T-shirts indoors in January and need light layers rather than heavy sweaters. Bring a humidifier or buy one: heated rooms drop to 20% humidity and dry skin, lips and throat are the standard first-month complaint.

What the northeast adds

Harbin and Changchun reach minus 25C or lower in January, and wind chill on campus makes the walk between dormitory and classroom the coldest part of the day. Add insulated trousers, wool socks, a face-covering scarf and thick rubber soles, because leather cracks in the cold.

Packing for the south: damp cold, no heating, mould risk

South of the Huai River there is no municipal heating, and indoor temperatures in January sit at 5-10C in Shanghai, Nanjing and Wuhan. Buildings shed heat, windows are single-glazed and the cold is humid, so the answer is layers plus a heated blanket, RMB 100-200 locally and the best purchase a Yangtze-zone student makes.

Mould is the other southern issue. The plum rain season runs from June to July in the Yangtze delta, humidity stays above 80% for weeks, and leather shoes, belts and camera lenses grow mould in a closed wardrobe. Bring silica packets, or buy a dehumidifier for RMB 300-600 if you stay for a full degree.

Summer in the furnace cities

Wuhan, Chongqing, Nanjing and Hangzhou regularly pass 38C in July and August, and the heat lasts until mid-September. Pack light cotton and linen, one pair of sandals, and a small umbrella, because the same months bring sudden thunderstorms. Air conditioning is standard in dormitories and classrooms, so a light cardigan matters for the temperature swing.

Documents and money for the first week

Keep the admission letter, the JW202 form, the passport with visa, the medical examination record, the insurance certificate and 10-20 passport photographs in your hand luggage, never in the checked bag. You will need the photographs for the residence permit, the campus card and the bank account.

Bring RMB 1,000-2,000 in cash for the first three days, because mobile payment needs a Chinese bank account and a Chinese phone number. Airport taxi, dormitory deposit and a SIM card are all easier in cash. Notify your bank that you are travelling, and carry a second card from a different issuer.

The document folder that saves a week

  • Passport, visa page and two photocopies of each
  • Admission letter and JW202 form, printed
  • Medical examination record and vaccination certificate
  • Insurance policy with an emergency phone number
  • Passport photographs, white background, 33 by 48 millimetres
  • Bank statement or scholarship letter, if your visa required proof of funds

Clothes sizes, shoes and what to buy in China instead

Chinese clothing sizes run one to two steps smaller than European or American equivalents, so a European large is often a Chinese XXL, and shoes above size 45 are hard to find outside specialist or online shops. Bring your own shoes if your feet are large, and buy clothing locally once you can try it on.

Bedding is the other item to buy after arrival: standard dormitory beds are 90 by 190 centimetres, so home-bought sheets do not fit. A full bedding set costs RMB 150-300 at Taobao, JD.com or the campus supermarket, delivered next day.

Electronics, medicine and the 220-volt check

China runs 220 volts at 50 hertz with Type A, C and I sockets. Laptops, phones and camera chargers marked 100-240V need only a plug adapter worth RMB 10; anything marked 120V only needs a transformer that costs more than replacing the appliance. Bring a power strip with your home plugs, which turns one adapter into four sockets.

Bring a two-month supply of any prescription medicine in original packaging with the prescription, because the same molecule may be sold under a different name, and some stimulants and strong painkillers are controlled. Paracetamol, ibuprofen, oral rehydration salts and plasters are cheap and easy to buy locally.

What to leave at home

Leave the kettle, the rice cooker, the hairdryer, the towels and the winter duvet. Each weighs two to four kilograms, each is replaced for under RMB 200 in China, and each occupies space that a year's worth of prescription medicine or a good pair of boots would use better.

The suit and the bookshelf

Leave the formal suit unless you have a specific defence, interview or conference in the calendar, because tailoring in China is inexpensive and fast. Leave the books, too: an English-language library plus Kindle covers the reading list, and 10 kilograms of paperbacks is USD 60-120 by air freight.
