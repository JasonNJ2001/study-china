How to Check a Chinese University Is Legitimate: MoE Lists, JW202 Forms and Agent Scams

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-19

Most bad outcomes in Chinese admissions are not caused by weak universities. They are caused by applicants paying a real university's money through an agent, or enrolling at an institution that was never accredited to take foreign students. Both are checkable in under an hour, using public registers rather than the brochure you were sent.

The one list that settles the question

China's Ministry of Education publishes an official list of institutions authorised to enrol international students, hosted on the education supervision information site at jsj.moe.edu.cn. If a school does not appear on that list, it cannot legally issue a student visa form, and any degree it sells you will not be recognised for further study or licensing at home.

The list is organised by province and updated periodically, so always check the current version rather than a screenshot an agent sends you. Search the institution's exact Chinese name, because close variants exist: a real university in one city and a privately run training centre using almost the same name are not the same legal entity.

What the list does not tell you

Appearing on the list means the institution may enrol foreign students. It says nothing about whether a specific programme is taught in English, whether the dormitory exists, or whether the degree is recognised in your country. Those are separate checks, and the next two sections cover them.

The 2020 nationality rule that catches applicants out

Since the Ministry of Education notice 教外来〔2020〕12号, applicants who acquired a foreign passport after holding Chinese nationality must have held that passport for at least four years and lived abroad for at least two of the last four years. Genuine foreign nationals are unaffected, but agents still sell "passport routes" that fail at document review.

What the JW202 form actually proves

The JW202 form, formally the Visa Application Form for Study in China, is generated by the university through the provincial education department and the ministry's international student system. Scholarship students receive JW201 instead. A university that cannot produce a JW202 cannot get you an X1 visa, so the form is the single most reliable proof that an offer is real.

Ask for the form number and compare the university name, programme and duration against your admission letter. A scanned "pre-admission letter" with a red stamp proves nothing, because stamps are trivially reproduced. If the sender's email is a Gmail or QQ address rather than a university domain ending in .edu.cn, treat the offer as unverified until the university's international office confirms it independently.

A five-minute verification table

Work through these five checks before you transfer any money, because each one has a public source and produces a specific answer rather than an impression. The table covers institution status, programme approval, visa-form authenticity, joint-programme approvals and degree verification. If any row fails, stop and ask the university's international office directly.

Red flags in agent conversations

Most scams follow a small number of scripts, and the six below account for the majority of complaints we see. Each one is designed to create urgency, transfer money to someone other than the university, or prevent you from contacting the institution directly. Recognising the pattern is usually enough, because scammers rarely vary the script.

  1. "Guaranteed admission" to a named university, with a fee that rises if your grades are lower.
  2. Payment requested to a personal account, a Hong Kong company, or a crypto wallet instead of the university's official account.
  3. A scholarship that requires you to pay a "processing fee" before the result is announced.
  4. Documents you never saw: an application submitted on your behalf with no portal login of your own.
  5. Pressure to accept within 48 hours, which exists to stop you from checking anything.
  6. A programme name that appears nowhere on the university's own website.

The test that separates agents from middlemen

A legitimate agent charges for service, not for admission, and will happily give you the university's international office contact so you can confirm the arrangement. Refusal to do that is the clearest signal available.

Checking the programme, not just the university

Chinese universities host two kinds of programme that often get confused: those the university runs itself, and Sino-foreign joint programmes approved by the ministry. Joint programmes carry an approval number and an expiry date in the crs.jsj.edu.cn register, and a programme that has expired or was never approved will not produce a degree you can use abroad.

If your offer is for a "2+2" or "3+1" arrangement, ask for the approval number in writing. Real joint programmes publish it on their own site; unapproved ones describe the partnership only in marketing language.

Verifying a degree you already hold

Chinese degrees are recorded in CHSI, the national higher education student information service, and employers or universities abroad usually want a CHSI report or a CSCSE credential evaluation rather than a notarised copy. Both take days rather than weeks if your certificate number is correct, and neither can be produced by an agent who does not have your actual enrolment record.

Why the first check protects the last one

This is also why the initial check matters: if you graduated from an institution that was never authorised to enrol foreigners, no verification channel will be able to confirm the award, and no amount of notarisation will fix it.

How much this should cost you

University application fees in China typically run CNY 400–800 per programme, paid directly through the university's portal or bank account. An agent's service fee is a separate commercial matter and can legitimately be several hundred US dollars for document handling and translation, but it should never be described as a payment to the university.

If the total quoted exceeds the published tuition plus a modest service fee, ask for a written breakdown naming each recipient. Agents who cannot produce one are usually charging for access they do not control.
