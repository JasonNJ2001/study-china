"Dormitory vs Off-Campus Housing in China: A Practical Guide"

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-10-27

For most international students the housing decision lands at the worst possible moment: a week before departure, visa in hand, no way to inspect a room. The university offers a bed in a dormitory. An agent offers an apartment. A friend of a friend offers a room in a shared flat three metro stops away. All three are legitimate options, and all three fail in different ways.

This guide compares them on the three things that actually decide the outcome: money, freedom and safety. It is written for students staying a semester or longer who do not have a local contact to vet a lease on their behalf.

Most students in this position face the same choice within two weeks of arrival, and the student accommodation china market splits cleanly into three tiers: the university dormitory, the shared apartment, and the studio rented alone.

What a Chinese university dormitory includes

Room types and what you share

Dormitory rooms are shared by default: four to six beds in older buildings, two to four in newer international student blocks. A bed, desk, wardrobe and air conditioning are standard. A private bathroom is not. Most buildings have shared washrooms per floor, and in some of them hot water runs on a fixed schedule rather than around the clock.

International student dormitories are often a separate building from the Chinese undergraduate halls. That is convenient for paperwork and quieter for study, but it also means less daily language practice than you might expect.

What you actually pay

Dormitory fees are charged per semester or per year and collected by the university, usually together with tuition. A shared room typically costs RMB 4,000 to 12,000 per year; a single room in an international block runs RMB 15,000 to 30,000. Electricity is metered separately in most buildings and split between roommates.

The advantage is not the price alone. It is that nothing else is required: no deposit, no contract in Chinese, no landlord, no utility accounts in your name. You arrive, you sign a registration form, you get a key.

Off-campus housing: what you gain

Rent, deposit and the paperwork nobody mentions

A one-bedroom flat near a city-centre campus runs RMB 2,500 to 6,000 a month; a room in a shared flat runs RMB 1,200 to 2,500. Beijing, Shanghai, Shenzhen and Hangzhou sit at the top of those ranges, while Xi'an, Chengdu, Chongqing and Changsha sit near the bottom.

Two costs surprise people. The deposit is normally one month's rent plus one month paid in advance, so three months of cash leaves your account before you move in. And most leases run twelve months, with the full amount often payable in one instalment rather than monthly.

The commute question

Chinese campuses are large. A flat that looks two kilometres away on a map can be a 25-minute walk to your teaching building once you are inside the gate. Measure the distance to your faculty building, not to the campus gate, and check whether the metro line you need is operating or still under construction.

The rule students break by accident

Anyone staying at a private address in China must register with the local police station within 24 hours of moving in. Hotels and dormitories do this automatically. A private landlord may not. Some will handle it, some will tell you it is unnecessary, and a few will quietly avoid it because the lease has not been declared for tax.

An unregistered address turns into a problem at visa renewal, when the exit-entry bureau asks for a registration slip covering your whole period of stay. Keep every slip. If a landlord refuses, ask the international student office to intervene — they handle this every term and usually know the local station by name.

Safety: what to check before you sign

  • Visit the building at night as well as at midday. Lighting, noise and who else lives on the floor only show up after dark.
  • Confirm the ownership document matches the name on the contract. Sublets from another tenant are common and legally fragile.
  • Ask whose name each utility account is in. An unpaid electricity bill attaches to the address, not to the person who ran up the debt.
  • Photograph every wall, appliance and meter reading before moving in, with the date visible in the frame.
  • Locate the fire exit and check whether smoke alarms work. Some older residential blocks have none.
  • Ask about internet installation. Certain buildings require a provider contract that takes two weeks to activate.

Which option suits which student

A semester-long exchange student should take the dormitory. The saving is real, and the administrative load — police registration, residence permit paperwork, utility accounts — is carried by people who have processed it thousands of times.

A degree student in the second year or later, who knows the city and has a Chinese-speaking friend, will usually be happier off campus. The freedom is genuine: cooking your own food, choosing your neighbours, no curfew and no shared washroom at 7 a.m.

A scholarship holder should read the accommodation terms first. Many scholarships cover a dormitory place but pay nothing towards rent, and some allow the accommodation allowance to be converted into a cash payment if you live elsewhere. That conversion is worth asking about explicitly, because the answer is rarely volunteered.

A decision sequence that works

  1. Confirm what your scholarship or university contract already includes before you look at a single listing.
  2. Spend the first semester in the dormitory. You cannot judge a neighbourhood you have never lived in.
  3. Use that semester to learn the city: which districts feel safe after 10 p.m., which metro lines reach your faculty, what a fair rent looks like.
  4. Move out at the start of a new academic year, when shared flats turn over and landlords are more willing to negotiate.
  5. Before signing anything, have the contract translated by someone who is not the agent showing you the flat.

The dormitory is not always the cheaper option once you count what a shared room in a cheaper district would cost. It is the low-risk option, and during your first months in a new country those two things are usually the same. Decide on what you can afford to lose if it goes wrong, not on the monthly figure alone. That is the calculation most students skip, and the one that costs the most to get wrong.
