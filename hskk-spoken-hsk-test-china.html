HSKK: The Spoken HSK Test Chinese Universities Now Ask For

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-11

HSKK is the speaking half of the HSK system, and it has quietly become the part that decides scholarship applications. A candidate with HSK 5 and no speaking score is, for some scholarship panels, less competitive than a candidate with HSK 4 and an HSKK intermediate certificate.

This guide covers what the test actually asks, how it is scored, where it is mandatory, and how to prepare when you have no language partner.

What HSKK is and who needs it

HSKK (汉语水平口语考试) is a standalone spoken Chinese test run by the same service as HSK, with three levels — beginner, intermediate and advanced — each scored out of 100 with 60 as the pass mark. It is optional for admission at most universities but mandatory for Confucius Institute Scholarship applicants and several Chinese-taught degree programmes.

The test is recorded and rated by trained assessors rather than machine-scored, which is worth knowing because it rewards intelligibility over accent imitation. You are not being marked against a native speaker; you are being marked on whether a Chinese listener understands you and whether your answer fits the question.

The three levels and what they demand

The levels map roughly onto HSK 1–2, HSK 3–4 and HSK 5–6, and each test lasts 20 to 25 minutes. Beginner asks you to repeat sentences, answer short questions and speak for about two minutes on a familiar topic; intermediate and advanced lengthen the response sections and add a read-aloud passage.

What beginner actually tests

Beginner is not a conversation. It tests whether you can reproduce tones and sentence patterns accurately under time pressure, so drilling recorded prompts out loud beats memorising vocabulary lists. A candidate who speaks slowly but correctly scores better than one who speaks fluently with broken tones.

Where intermediate becomes hard

Intermediate adds the read-aloud section, which exposes pronunciation you can hide in free speech, and a two-minute response that has to be structured rather than listed. Most failures at this level come from answers that run out of content after 40 seconds.

How the test is scored

Raters score pronunciation, fluency and content coherence, and the whole session is audio-recorded for moderation. The result is a single number out of 100 rather than a band, and the certificate names the level you sat, so a 65 at intermediate is not interchangeable with a 65 at advanced when a scholarship table asks for a specific level.

Because scoring is human, tone errors that impede meaning cost more than a regional accent that does not. Candidates from Vietnam or Indonesia who speak with a strong accent but clear tones routinely score above candidates who sound more native but swallow final consonants.

Where HSKK is actually required

HSKK is required, not merely recommended, in three places: the Confucius Institute Scholarship, which pairs HSK 3 with HSKK beginner for a one-year language course and HSK 4 with HSKK intermediate for a degree; several Chinese-taught undergraduate programmes in medicine and education; and a growing number of provincial scholarship schemes that copied the national template.

Reading the requirement table correctly

Scholarship tables list HSK and HSKK scores in the same row, and the levels must match: an HSK 4 with an HSKK beginner score does not satisfy a requirement written as HSK 4 plus HSKK intermediate. Check the wording before you book a test, because retaking at the wrong level wastes a full cycle.

Registration, dates and cost

Registration runs through the official Chinese test service, and tests are scheduled roughly monthly at Confucius Institutes and university test centres, with heavier calendars in March, May, September and December. Fees are around 200 RMB at beginner level and 300 to 400 RMB at intermediate and advanced, paid online when you register.

Booking practicalities

Registration usually closes two to four weeks before the test date, and paper-based sittings at smaller centres fill quickly. Bring the passport you registered with, arrive 30 minutes early for the identity check, and confirm the centre's address in Chinese before travelling, since many sit inside a university building with no English signage.

Preparing without a language partner

Most candidates prepare alone, and the constraint is speaking time rather than materials. The workable routine is to record yourself answering timed prompts, then listen back against the transcript — a method that costs nothing and reproduces the test's actual conditions.

  • Record 20 beginner prompts a day and compare your tone contour with the model audio.
  • Time every answer with a stopwatch; two minutes is longer than it feels.
  • Read aloud for 10 minutes daily to train the read-aloud section.
  • Re-record the same prompt after a week and compare, rather than moving to new material.
  • Book one 25-minute session a week with a tutor or a language-exchange partner for correction.

Retakes and score validity

HSKK scores are valid for two years from the test date, and there is no limit on retakes: you can sit again the following month if a centre near you has space. Most candidates gain 10 to 15 points on a second attempt purely from familiarity with the recording format and the timing of each section.

Plan backwards from the scholarship deadline rather than from the test calendar. A score taken in September is safely inside the validity window for a March application, while a score from three years ago is not, and re-sitting costs less than losing an award on a technicality.
