"Getting a SIM Card and Internet Access in China as a Student"

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-08

Nothing in China works without a phone number. Lecture notices arrive in WeChat groups, parcels wait in lockers that open by SMS code, and the bank will not open an account until a +86 number is registered in your own name. Buying that SIM is a twenty-minute task with a thirty-minute queue attached, and where you do it changes the price by a factor of three.

Do you actually need a Chinese phone number?

Yes, and a roaming SIM from home will not substitute. Chinese services verify identity by sending a code to a domestic number, so Alipay, WeChat Pay, banking apps, delivery platforms and campus Wi-Fi portals all reject foreign numbers at registration. The number is also what the university uses to reach you when something urgent happens.

The practical consequence is that the SIM is your first purchase, before the bank account, before the metro card, and ideally on the day you land. Everything else in the settlement sequence depends on it.

Which carrier should you choose?

China TelecomStable broadband bundles with a phone lineContract terms tie phone and home internet

China Mobile has the largest network, with coverage that holds up on trains and in mountain counties where the other two drop. China Unicom competes on data volume and is usually the cheapest for a student who streams video. China Telecom bundles well if your dormitory uses its fibre, because one contract then covers both the phone and the room.

How much does a plan cost?

Expect 30 to 60 RMB per month for 20 to 60 GB of data on a prepaid or short contract, and less on a campus plan sold through the university. A 12-month contract typically doubles the data allowance for the same money, at the cost of an early-termination fee if you leave early.

Airport counters are the exception. A tourist SIM sold at arrivals commonly costs 100 to 200 RMB for a month of limited data, and the same carrier's business hall near campus sells a better plan for a third of the price.

Prepaid versus contract

Prepaid needs no credit check and can be cancelled by simply not topping up, which suits exchange students staying one semester. Contract plans are cheaper per gigabyte and often include a subsidised handset, but they require a residence permit and a bank card, and they penalise early exit.

Where should you buy the SIM?

Buy it at an official carrier business hall (营业厅) with your passport, not from a reseller, a convenience shop or a stranger in a dormitory group chat. Since 2015 every Chinese mobile number must be registered against a real identity, and a number registered to somebody else can be suspended without warning, taking your banking app with it.

Why the passport name must match

Bring the passport you used to enter the country, because the name on the registration must match the name on the visa. Branches take walk-ins, though the queue at a campus-adjacent hall in September can run to an hour; going at opening time on a weekday cuts that to ten minutes.

What is campus internet like?

Most Chinese universities provide both wired dormitory internet and campus Wi-Fi, and both usually need a student number rather than a phone number. Wired access is the more reliable option for research work, while campus Wi-Fi covers classrooms and libraries but slows sharply in the evening when the whole cohort is streaming.

Dormitory broadband typically costs 30 to 60 RMB per month for a capped package, sometimes billed by gigabyte after the cap, and installation requires a student card plus a room contract. Campus Wi-Fi login portals differ by university; the international office or a senior student in your building can supply the exact steps faster than any guide.

Which websites are blocked, and how does that affect study?

A defined set of foreign services is not reachable from mainland networks, including Google, YouTube, Instagram, Facebook, WhatsApp and X. The practical impact on a student is narrower than the list suggests: Baidu and Bing work for search, WeChat replaces WhatsApp, Bilibili and Youku replace YouTube, and Chinese universities mirror most academic databases through their own library portals.

Academic access through the library

The real friction appears in research. Google Scholar and many publisher sites are unreachable, so use the library's proxy, CNKI for Chinese sources, and the university's own document delivery service. Ask the library for its off-campus access route in the first week; it is the legitimate channel and it is free.

VPN use and the legal position

Selling or operating an unauthorised VPN is illegal in China under Ministry of Industry and Information Technology rules, and enforcement targets providers rather than individual users. That distinction matters for how you behave: installing a commercial VPN for personal use is a grey area with real risk, while using your university's sanctioned academic access is straightforwardly legal.

What are the rules and traps?

Five rules cover almost every SIM card problem: register the number to your own passport, keep the physical card safe, read the contract length before signing, activate roaming before leaving China, and settle any balance before departure. Skipping any of them produces a lockout that costs more time to fix than the original purchase took.

  1. Register the number to your own passport; never buy a pre-registered SIM.
  2. Keep the physical SIM safe, because replacing it requires the original passport at a business hall.
  3. Read the contract length before signing; a twelve-month plan sold in June can outlive your programme.
  4. Activate international roaming before travelling home, or you will lose SMS codes abroad.
  5. Cancel or downgrade the plan before departure, since unpaid balances follow the passport number.

The second-hand SIM market deserves special caution. Numbers are recycled after inactivity, and a recycled number can still be linked to previous owners' accounts, which means verification codes for their services arriving on your phone. A fresh number from an official hall avoids that entirely.

Treat the SIM as the first item on your arrival checklist rather than an afterthought: one passport, one business hall, one plan sized to your data habits, and the rest of the settlement process opens up from there.
