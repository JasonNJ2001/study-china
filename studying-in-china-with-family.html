"Studying in China with Your Family: Spouse and Child Visas"

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-07

Most countries issue a dependant visa that follows a student visa automatically. China does not. Family members of an international student enter on a private visit visa, convert it to a residence permit after arrival, and live with a status that permits residence but not employment. The rules are workable, but they reward planning six months ahead and punish anyone who assumes the family can simply travel on a tourist visa and sort it out later.

Is there a dependant visa for students in China?

There is no dependant visa class for students. A spouse or child of an international student applies for an S1 or S2 private visit visa instead: S1 for a stay longer than 180 days, S2 for 180 days or less. The application needs an invitation from the student plus notarised proof of relationship. Neither visa allows the holder to work, and neither converts automatically into anything else.

The practical consequence is that the family's legal status is tied to the student's, and it can only be applied for once the student holds a valid residence permit rather than a single-entry X1 visa. Chinese consulates generally want a copy of that permit, which means the family usually arrives a semester after the student.

The S1 and S2 private visit visas

S1 is the long-stay route and the one families should target. It is issued for the duration of the student's residence permit, up to a year at a time, and it is renewable inside China without leaving. S2 is the short-stay version, valid for up to 180 days, and it cannot be extended into a residence permit for a long stay without a trip out and a fresh application.

What the invitation letter must contain

The invitation is issued by the university's international office, not by the student personally, although a personal letter is usually required alongside it. It must state the student's name, passport number, programme, the duration of study, the family member's details, the purpose of the visit and who covers costs. A letter that omits the funding statement is the most common reason for a refusal at the consulate window.

Proof of relationship

Marriage and birth certificates must be notarised and translated, and in some countries legalised or apostilled before the Chinese consulate will accept them. A translation done by the family is refused; it has to come from a recognised translation service with a stamp. Allow four to six weeks for this step alone, and note that the certificates must match the names on the passports exactly, including middle names.

The 30-day conversion after arrival

An S1 visa is a single-entry document, not a residence permit. Within 30 days of entry the holder must attend the local exit-entry administration with the university's registration form, a health certificate, photographs and the accommodation registration slip, and apply for a residence permit for private purposes. Missing the 30-day window means a fine of RMB 500 per day, capped at RMB 10,000, and possible detention.

Accommodation registration is the step that trips families up. Every foreigner must be registered at the local police station within 24 hours of moving into a private flat, and the landlord has to attend with the lease and property certificate. Hotels do it automatically; rented flats do not.

Housing: why the dormitory will not work

University dormitories are allocated per registered student, in single or shared rooms, and they do not accommodate a spouse and children. A family therefore rents off campus, which in a tier-one city means RMB 4,000 to 8,000 a month for a two-bedroom flat within commuting distance of campus, and RMB 2,000 to 4,000 in a city such as Nanjing, Wuhan or Chengdu.

What a lease requires from a foreign tenant

Most landlords ask for three months' deposit, one month in advance and a copy of the residence permit, and many refuse foreign passports because police registration is extra work. An agent who handles the registration, for roughly half a month's rent, is faster than negotiating directly, and the lease should name every family member living there.

Schooling for children

Children of foreign residents have no automatic right to a place in a Chinese public school. Access depends on the city and the district, usually requires Chinese language ability, and often requires a residence permit for the child plus proof of local address. Where places exist they are cheap, sometimes free of tuition, and the child is immersed in Chinese within a year.

International versus public schools

International schools are the reliable option and the expensive one, at RMB 60,000 to 200,000 a year depending on the city and the curriculum. They teach in English or in a foreign national curriculum, they accept foreign passports without a language test, and they run the full age range. Bilingual private schools sit between the two at RMB 30,000 to 80,000 and teach a Chinese curriculum with more English.

Health insurance and the coverage gap

The compulsory insurance bundled with a CSC scholarship, priced at around RMB 800 a year, covers the enrolled student only. It does not extend to a spouse or child, and it does not cover maternity care or routine check-ups. Dependants need their own policy, bought either locally through a Chinese insurer such as Ping An or PICC, or internationally through a provider that operates in mainland China.

  • Confirm the policy covers inpatient care in Chinese hospitals, not only overseas
  • Check whether direct billing is available, or reimbursement only
  • Check the maternity waiting period, which is often 10 to 12 months
  • Keep every receipt: reimbursement requires the original invoice and diagnosis
  • Register the policy with the university so the clinic has a record

A realistic timeline and budget

Start the family's paperwork when the student's residence permit is issued, not before, because the permit number goes into the invitation letter. Notarisation takes four to six weeks, the visa appointment another two to four, and the family should arrive at a semester boundary so children start school at the beginning of a term.

Budget RMB 100,000 to 150,000 a year for a spouse and one child in a tier-two city, covering rent, food, insurance and a mid-range school, and roughly double in Shanghai or Beijing. Students weighing a family move against a solo year abroad can check programme costs and city comparisons before committing.
