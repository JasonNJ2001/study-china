Textbooks, Printing and Study Materials in China: What You Actually Pay

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-17

Study materials are the smallest line in most international students' Chinese budget, and also the one that surprises them most, because the pattern is the opposite of home: books are cheap, printing is constant, and the largest single bill usually arrives in the final year for a thesis.

What Course Materials Actually Cost Per Year

A full year of textbooks, printing and stationery for an undergraduate at a Chinese university typically lands between ¥700 and ¥1,600, depending on subject. Textbooks themselves are the small part, at ¥25–60 each for a state-published title, while photocopying, lab notebooks and printing of assignments make up the recurring bulk of the bill across two semesters.

Why the books are cheap

Chinese academic textbooks are cheap for structural reasons. Major titles come from state publishers such as Higher Education Press (高等教育出版社), print runs run into the hundreds of thousands, and the Ministry of Education has historically kept a price ceiling on compulsory undergraduate textbooks. A first-year calculus text that would cost £45 in the UK is ¥42 in China, and it is the same edition used by the whole cohort.

Printing Is the Recurring Cost Nobody Budgets For

Campus print shops (文印店) charge about ¥0.10 per A4 page for single-sided black and white and ¥0.15 for double-sided, with A3 drawings at ¥0.50–1.00. That sounds trivial until a course requires a weekly 20-page handout: 16 weeks of handouts, lab reports and a group project come to ¥80–150 per course, and engineering or design students print far more.

Where printing hides in the timetable

The heaviest printing months are September and December, when lecture notes, past papers and revision packs are distributed as PDFs rather than books. Many lecturers no longer order a textbook at all and post scanned chapters instead, which shifts the cost from the bookshop to the print shop. Budget ¥200 per semester for printing if your course is text-heavy.

Binding and submission

Final-year theses are printed and bound, usually three copies, at ¥15–40 per copy with a hard cover and ¥8–15 for a soft comb binding. Some faculties specify a template, a cover colour and a spiral type, so the campus shop nearest the faculty building is usually the one that knows the requirement and will not reject your format.

Second-Hand and Library Routes

Second-hand textbooks circulate heavily. Campus WeChat groups, graduating students in June, and platforms like Xianyu (闲鱼) sell last year's edition at 30–50 percent of list price, and the content is usually unchanged. The library also subscribes to Chinese ebook platforms including Chaoxing (超星) and Duxiu (读秀), which let you read a scanned copy online for free with your student login.

The edition trap

Chinese textbooks are revised frequently, and a course may specify the nth edition with a different chapter order. Check the syllabus for the edition number before buying second-hand, because a ¥20 saving against a ¥45 book is poor value if the assignment questions come from a chapter that moved. Ask the teaching assistant, not the seller.

Reference books you should not buy

Dictionaries, style guides and national standards are almost all available through the library's database subscriptions, and Chinese academic libraries license CNKI, Wanfang and the national standards database (国家标准全文公开系统) for on-campus use. Downloading a standard from a paid site when your library already licenses it is the most common avoidable expense for engineering students.

Costs by Subject

The spread between faculties is wide, and it is driven by printing volume and equipment rather than by book prices. Medicine and engineering sit at the top because of atlases, drawing kits and lab reports; computer science sits at the bottom because tooling is free and handouts are digital. The 教材费 Prepayment and How It Is Settled

Many universities collect a textbook fee (教材费) with tuition, usually ¥500–800 per year for undergraduates, and then settle the actual cost at the end of the year. If the real spend is lower, the difference is refunded or carried forward; if it is higher, you are billed the balance. Ask the finance office for the settlement sheet, because the prepayment is not a flat charge and unused money is refundable.

When the books are actually handed out

Textbook distribution normally happens in the first two weeks of term at a faculty collection point, against your student card. If you arrive after the distribution window, you will be sent to the campus bookshop and may pay list price. Students who register late should email the faculty office before arrival and ask them to hold the set.

Six Ways to Cut the Bill

The savings are real but modest, and they come from process rather than from hunting for bargains. Second-hand buying, double-sided printing, the library's database access and the faculty's binding template together remove most of the avoidable spend without touching the compulsory items. Work through the list in the order given, since the first two matter most:

  1. Buy second-hand from graduating students in June, not from the bookshop in September.
  2. Confirm the edition number on the syllabus before paying for any used copy.
  3. Print double-sided, and combine several weeks of handouts into one print job.
  4. Use the library's CNKI, Duxiu and standards access instead of buying reference works.
  5. Keep a PDF folder of past papers rather than reprinting them each December.
  6. For the thesis, ask the faculty for the approved binding template before ordering three copies.

None of these changes the size of a Chinese study budget the way tuition or accommodation does, but together they cover most of a semester's living costs for a careful student.
