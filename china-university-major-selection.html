"How to Read a Chinese University Course Catalogue and Pick Your Major"

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-10

The English name on the brochure and the Chinese name on the admission letter are not always the same degree. Chinese universities publish a standardised catalogue, and the major code it assigns decides which courses you take, which credits you need and what your transcript will say for the rest of your career.

How is a Chinese university course catalogue organised?

The Ministry of Education catalogue sorts every degree into 12 discipline gates, 93 major categories and more than 800 individual majors, each carrying a code that universities must use. Your admission letter names one of those majors, and the training programme (培养方案) attached to it fixes your credits, compulsory courses and graduation requirements.

That structure is the reason two universities in different provinces can offer a degree with the same name and almost the same curriculum. The catalogue is a national standard, and a university deviates from it only by adding electives or track options inside the code it holds.

The three levels of the catalogue

  • Discipline gate (学科门类): engineering, science, economics, medicine, literature and seven others.
  • Major category (专业类): the family, for example Computer Science, Mechanical Engineering, Business Administration.
  • Individual major (专业): the degree on your admission letter, with its own code and curriculum.

The translation trap that catches applicants

Major names do not translate cleanly, and three examples catch people every year. Information and Computing Science (信息与计算科学) is a mathematics degree, not computer science. Automation (自动化) sits closer to control engineering than to robotics. Business Administration often carries more accounting than management.

The rule is simple: read the compulsory course list, not the English title. If the first-year modules are calculus, linear algebra and probability, you have applied to a mathematics programme whatever the brochure calls it.

What the training programme tells you

The programme document lists total credits, the split between compulsory and elective, the language of instruction for each course, and the thesis requirement. Undergraduate degrees usually require 140-170 credits over four years, and medicine, architecture and some engineering programmes run five years instead of four.

Read it before you accept the offer, not after you arrive. It is published on the school's Chinese website far more often than on the English one, and it names the courses that decide whether the degree matches what you think you applied for.

Where to find it

Search the university site for 培养方案 plus your major name, or ask the international office for the current cohort's PDF. Older versions are still online and differ: credit totals and course names change every few years, so check the year in the file name.

Chinese-taught versus English-taught tracks

Many universities run both tracks for the same major, and they are not equivalent. The English track typically has a smaller elective pool, different tuition, a separate class cohort and fewer Chinese classmates, and switching to the Chinese track later requires HSK 5 and a formal transfer approved by both schools.

The tuition difference is significant, often RMB 10,000-20,000 a year more for the English track. The Chinese track is cheaper and better for language acquisition, but it assumes you can follow a lecture on partial differential equations in Mandarin from week one.

Changing your major after arrival

Major changes (转专业) are possible but tightly regulated. Most universities allow one application, in the first or second year, require a GPA threshold such as the top 20-30% of your cohort, and cap the number of transfers per school. International students also need the international office to sign.

A transfer usually costs a year, because the new programme's first-year courses were not taken. Some universities allow credit recognition for shared foundational modules, which is why transferring in the second semester of year one is cheaper than transferring in year two.

What to prepare for a transfer request

  1. Your transcript showing a GPA above the school's threshold.
  2. A written statement explaining the academic reason, not the personal one.
  3. A letter from the receiving school confirming capacity.
  4. Approval from the international office, in writing, before the deadline.

Choosing between similar majors

  1. Compare the compulsory course list, not the name, and count how many modules are mathematics.
  2. Check which school the major sits in: the same name under a different faculty has a different curriculum.
  3. Ask about laboratory access and whether undergraduates can join a professor's project before year four.
  4. Confirm the language of instruction for each core course, not just for the programme.
  5. Look at where graduates go, and ask the school for the last two years of destination data.
  6. Verify the credit total and the thesis requirement, because both change the workload substantially.

Before you accept the offer

Ask three questions in writing and keep the answers. Which catalogue code does this major carry, what are the first-year compulsory courses, and what is the language of instruction for each of them. A university that answers all three clearly is a university that knows its own programme, and that is a better signal than any ranking table.
