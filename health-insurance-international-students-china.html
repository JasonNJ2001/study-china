Health Insurance for International Students in China

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-03

Health insurance is one of the few requirements in the Chinese student visa process that is both mandatory and badly explained. You need a policy to convert an X1 visa into a residence permit, most universities fold a group plan into your registration fee, and almost nobody reads the terms until they are standing at a hospital cashier holding a bill.

Here is what the coverage actually looks like, where it stops, and what a sensible top-up costs.

Why you cannot skip it

The residence permit application at the municipal exit-entry hall requires proof of medical insurance covering your stay in China. A travel policy bought for the flight does not satisfy this in most cities, because it expires before your programme does.

Universities solve this by enrolling every international student in a group scheme, usually the national student insurance programme administered through the campus clinic. The premium is typically 600 to 1,000 yuan a year and is charged with tuition. Some universities make it optional if you can prove equivalent cover, but proving equivalence means a Chinese-language policy document naming a Chinese insurer, which is more work than simply joining.

The three policy types you will meet

Almost every student ends up with one of three arrangements, and they behave very differently when you actually need care.

The university group plan

This is the default. It is cheap, it is accepted everywhere, and it is built around the campus clinic. You go to the clinic first, get a referral if the case is beyond them, and then claim back the hospital bill. Reimbursement rates for inpatient care are usually 70 to 90 per cent after a deductible; outpatient visits are capped at a few hundred yuan a year, which is enough for a cold and not enough for anything chronic.

The commercial plan

A commercial student policy costs two to four times more and buys three things the group plan does not: direct billing at private and international departments, higher outpatient limits, and English-speaking customer service. If you have a condition that needs regular monitoring, this is usually worth the money. Read the network hospital list for your specific city before you buy, because a plan that only bills directly at hospitals in Beijing is not much use in Nanjing.

The travel policy mistake

Students frequently arrive with a ninety-day travel policy from home and assume it converts. It does not. It will get you through the airport and possibly through enrolment, and then it expires in the middle of your first semester, which is exactly when the residence permit renewal is checked.

What is covered, and what quietly is not

Covered, in broad terms: inpatient treatment, surgery, emergency care, prescription drugs on the approved list, and outpatient visits within the annual cap.

Not covered, in almost every plan sold to students:

  • Dental treatment, including fillings and root canals. Cleaning and extraction are sometimes partly covered; anything restorative is not.
  • Vision correction, glasses and contact lenses.
  • Routine physical examinations that are not part of the visa health check.
  • Pre-existing conditions during the first months of cover, and pregnancy in many group plans.
  • Mental health treatment beyond a handful of counselling sessions, if anything.
  • Treatment received outside mainland China.
  • Injuries from what the policy calls risky activity: skiing, motorcycling, contact sports, and in some policies, cycling without a helmet.

The gap that surprises people: dental and vision

A single root canal in a Chinese tier-one city costs 1,500 to 3,000 yuan. A pair of prescription glasses, made locally, costs 200 to 600 yuan and is cheap enough that most students simply pay. Dental is the one worth planning for. If you expect work during your programme, do it before you leave home or budget for it explicitly.

How a claim actually works

The process is administrative and depends entirely on which hospital you choose.

Direct billing versus reimbursement

At a hospital in your insurer's network, you show your insurance card at the cashier and pay only the uncovered share. Outside the network, you pay the full bill and file for reimbursement later, which means keeping the original invoice, the itemised treatment list, the diagnosis certificate and a copy of your passport, all stamped by the hospital. Missing one stamped document is the most common reason a claim is rejected.

The campus clinic is the gatekeeper

In most universities the clinic must see you first, or at least issue the referral, for the group plan to pay. Going straight to a large hospital without that referral can cut your reimbursement rate. It is not a medical judgement, it is a paperwork rule, and the clinic staff will explain it if you ask on day one rather than on the day you are ill.

Five things to do before you fly

  1. Get a copy of your home medical record, in English, listing any chronic condition and current medication.
  2. Bring at least three months of any prescription you take regularly, in original packaging with the prescription attached.
  3. Photograph your home insurance policy and its emergency contact number, and store it offline.
  4. Ask your university in writing which insurer they use and whether the plan includes outpatient cover.
  5. Budget 1,000 to 2,000 yuan a year as a medical buffer, separate from tuition and living costs.

The realistic cost of getting it wrong

An appendicitis operation with a week in hospital in a Chinese public hospital runs 15,000 to 30,000 yuan before insurance. Under the group plan, most of that comes back. Under an expired travel policy, none of it does. The difference between the two outcomes is a form you sign during enrolment week, which is why the paperwork deserves ten minutes of attention rather than a signature at the bottom of a stack.

If you are mapping out the full cost of a programme and want the medical line item placed alongside tuition and housing, that planning is easier to do before the offer deadline than after arrival.
