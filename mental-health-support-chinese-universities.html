"Mental Health Support at Chinese Universities: Counselling, Insurance and Stigma"

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-07

Chinese universities are required to provide mental health services, and the requirement is not decorative: every institution must operate a counselling centre, screen incoming students and keep full-time staff on payroll. What varies enormously is language, waiting time and what happens after the first appointment. International students often discover the gap between the regulation and the practice only when they need it, which is the wrong moment to start researching.

How university counselling actually works in China

Every Chinese university must maintain a mental health education centre under the Ministry of Education's 2011 construction standard for student psychological services, and the service is free to enrolled students. Sessions are usually 50 minutes, booked through the centre or a online form, and the standard allocation is six to eight sessions per academic year. Referral to a hospital follows if the centre judges the case beyond its scope.

The centre sits inside the student affairs structure, which means it is staffed by counsellors employed by the university rather than by an independent clinic. That structure has advantages, mainly cost and access, and one disadvantage: the counsellor is also an employee of the institution whose rules you live under.

What the first-semester screening involves

New students normally complete a psychological survey in the first weeks, using instruments such as the UPI or SCL-90, and students whose scores cross a threshold are invited for an interview. The screening is not diagnostic and it is not used to expel anyone, but the interview is on the record inside the centre. Answering honestly is the sensible course; the alternative is that a real problem stays invisible until it is acute.

Language is the real barrier

Counselling in China happens in Mandarin almost everywhere, and the quality of the encounter depends on your Chinese far more than on the counsellor's training. A student at HSK 4 can discuss coursework and housing but usually cannot describe intrusive thoughts or family history with the precision therapy requires. Comprehensive universities in Shanghai, Beijing and Guangzhou increasingly keep one or two English-speaking counsellors, but availability is measured in hours per week, not sessions per day.

The workaround most students use is to combine the two: keep the campus centre for administrative support, leave of absence paperwork and referrals, and pay for English-language therapy separately. Arrange it in the first month rather than the first crisis.

What CSC insurance does and does not cover

The comprehensive medical insurance required of X1 holders, priced at around RMB 800 a year and arranged through the China Scholarship Council for scholarship students, is built around accidents and hospitalisation. It reimburses inpatient care, surgery, and outpatient treatment for injury or acute illness at 85 percent within the scheme's limits. It does not reimburse routine psychotherapy, long-term psychiatric medication, or treatment for pre-existing conditions.

Commercial international plans cover some of it, typically 20 to 50 sessions a year after a waiting period, and cost USD 1,000 to 3,000 annually.

The gap that matters most

Medication is where the shortfall bites. Psychiatric drugs are cheap by Western standards at RMB 100 to 400 a month, but they need a hospital prescription renewed every four to twelve weeks, and a student who leaves the city for the summer needs a doctor's letter to avoid a break in treatment.

Hospitals and private therapy: cost and access

Public psychiatric care is concentrated in specialist hospitals, and the reputable ones are busy and used to foreign patients. Beijing Anding Hospital, the Shanghai Mental Health Center and the West China Hospital in Chengdu all run outpatient psychiatry with app-based registration, the realistic route to an appointment. A senior physician registration costs RMB 100 to 300, and the queue runs to days.

  • Register through the hospital's official WeChat account or app, not a third-party agent
  • Bring your passport and residence permit to every appointment
  • Ask for the diagnosis certificate (诊断证明) if you need it for a leave of absence
  • Keep every invoice; reimbursement requires the original
  • Book the follow-up before you leave the building, because slots open two weeks out

Private practice and online options

Private clinics in tier-one cities employ English-speaking psychiatrists and therapists, charge RMB 600 to 1,500 for an initial assessment, and can usually see a patient within a week. Online platforms licensed in mainland China offer video sessions from RMB 200, but psychiatric drugs still require a hospital visit, since online first prescriptions are restricted.

Confidentiality, family and the stigma question

Counselling records are confidential, with the standard exceptions: risk of serious harm to yourself or someone else, and disclosure ordered by a court. In practice, the boundary that surprises foreign students is the university's duty of care. If the centre judges a student to be at risk, it may inform the school's student affairs office and, in the case of a hospital admission, the student's emergency contact.

When the university contacts your family

The usual trigger is a hospital admission or a documented suicide risk, not a routine session. Chinese universities operate under a safety-first administrative culture, and the international office is expected to inform the family and the embassy in serious cases. Students who want to control that conversation should name their preferred contact in writing at registration and tell the counsellor who it is.

Stigma is narrower than it looks from outside: among students, seeing a counsellor is unremarkable, while some families still weigh a diagnosis heavily. The national hotline 12356 has been rolled out across provinces since 2025 and takes calls in Mandarin.

How to build support before you need it

The students who cope best treat mental health as logistics rather than emergency. They identify an English-speaking clinician in the first month, buy insurance that names mental health explicitly, and tell one person on campus how to reach their family. That preparation costs a few hours and removes the worst part of a bad week, which is not knowing who to call.

  • Save the campus centre's address and opening hours in your phone
  • Ask the international office, in writing, which counsellors speak English
  • Check whether your policy covers outpatient psychotherapy and what the annual cap is
  • Register with a hospital psychiatry department before you need it
  • Decide who your emergency contact is and confirm they agree

Applicants comparing universities on cost and ranking can add student support to the checklist, because the difference between a campus with an English-speaking counsellor and one without is larger than most prospectuses admit.
