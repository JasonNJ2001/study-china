What Campus Food Really Costs in China: Canteens, Cards and Budgets

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-20

Food is the one budget line where China is genuinely cheap, and the gap between eating on campus and eating like a visitor is wider than most students expect. A canteen lunch costs less than a coffee in Seoul. The catch is that the cheap food sits behind a card system, a timetable and a queue, and the students who overspend are almost always the ones who never learned the canteen rhythm.

What a canteen meal actually costs

A standard canteen meal at a Chinese public university costs CNY 8–15: two vegetable dishes, one meat dish and unlimited rice, priced per dish rather than per set. Breakfast runs CNY 4–8, with steamed buns at CNY 1–3 each and soy milk at CNY 2–3. Prices barely move between cities, because canteens are cost-controlled rather than market-priced.

The variation that does exist is between tiers within the same building. Most canteens run a basic line (基本伙食) at the lowest price, a speciality line with regional dishes at CNY 12–20, and a small Muslim or Western counter. Students who eat from the basic line every day spend roughly half what students who drift to the speciality counters spend, for broadly similar nutrition.

Breakfast, lunch and dinner are three different budgets

Breakfast is the cheapest meal by a wide margin: congee, buns and soy milk together cost CNY 5–8, and skipping it is why many students overspend at 10 a.m. Dinner is the most expensive, because canteens run fewer cheap dishes in the evening and students are more likely to order in.

Why campus food is cheaper than the street

University canteens operate on a different economic model from restaurants: the building is provided by the university, staff are often university employees, and many institutions run the basic line at or near cost as part of student welfare. That is why a dish that costs CNY 22 across the road costs CNY 9 inside the gate.

The subsidy is also why canteens have rules that surprise foreigners. Portions are standardised, cards are required, and outside food is sometimes discouraged in peak hours. Canteens also close sharply between service periods, typically 09:00–11:00 and 13:30–16:30, which means a 3 p.m. hunger pang sends you to a convenience store at triple the unit price.

The campus card is the wallet that matters

The campus card (一卡通) is a stored-value card that pays for canteen meals, printing, laundry, gym entry and sometimes the campus bus. It is topped up at machines near the canteen entrance or through the university's WeChat account, and the balance is visible in the same app. Many canteens now also accept WeChat Pay and Alipay, but the card remains faster in a queue and is the only option at some counters.

Losing the card is a routine event and rarely a disaster: it is tied to your student number, so a replacement at the card centre costs CNY 20–30 and the balance transfers. The genuine risk is lending it out, because canteen subsidies are meant for enrolled students and some universities suspend cards used by non-students.

Topping up, freezing and replacing a lost card

Top-up machines beside the canteen accept banknotes, but the university's WeChat account is faster and lets you freeze a lost card immediately. Freeze it before reporting it, because the balance is spendable by anyone who finds it while it is still active.

Delivery apps and the true cost of convenience

Meituan and Ele.me dominate food delivery on and around Chinese campuses, and they are cheaper than delivery in Europe but not cheap relative to the canteen. A typical order lands at CNY 25–40 including a delivery fee of CNY 2–6, which is two to three canteen meals for one portion of noodles.

The deeper cost is timing. Security often blocks riders at the gate, so deliveries meet students at a pickup shelf, and a lunchtime order placed at 11:45 can arrive at 12:40. Delivery makes sense for late nights and exam weeks; as a daily habit it adds CNY 500–800 a month.

Why riders stop at the gate

Most campuses bar outside riders at the gate, and larger ones restrict them entirely from dormitory zones. The result is a pickup shelf near the gate, a two-minute walk each way, and food that has cooled by the time it reaches your room, which is a quiet argument for walking to the canteen instead.

Halal, vegetarian and dietary constraints

Most universities with a significant Muslim student population run a dedicated halal canteen (清真食堂), often a separate building or a segregated section with its own kitchen and staff. It is usually the cheapest halal food in the city, and it is the reason many students from Central Asia, Pakistan and West Africa choose one university over another.

Vegetarians have a harder time, because Chinese home cooking uses pork fat, dried shrimp and oyster sauce in dishes that look vegetable-based. The reliable strategy is to order from the vegetable counter and ask for 素 (vegetarian) explicitly, or to cook. Vegan students should expect to self-cater at least half their meals, and dormitory kitchen access varies sharply by university.

Cooking in a dormitory

Shared kitchens exist in some dormitory blocks and are banned in others, and enforcement depends on the building manager rather than a written rule. An electric rice cooker is the common workaround because it draws low power, and it is also the appliance most likely to be confiscated during an inspection.

A scholarship stipend of CNY 2,500–3,500 a month covers food comfortably at the second level. The students who run out of money in week three are usually at the fourth level without realising it, because each individual order felt small.

Five habits that cut the bill

  1. Eat breakfast in the canteen; it is the cheapest meal of the day and the one most often skipped.
  2. Learn the basic line's serving hours and arrive in the first thirty minutes, when portions are largest.
  3. Keep CNY 100–200 on the campus card so a queue never forces a delivery order.
  4. Buy fruit and yoghurt from the campus supermarket rather than a convenience store.
  5. Cook one meal a day if your dormitory allows it; a rice cooker pays for itself in three weeks.

None of this requires deprivation. It requires knowing that the cheap option exists, where it is, and when it closes, which is information no orientation session provides.
