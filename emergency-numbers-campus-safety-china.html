Emergency Numbers and Campus Safety in China

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-25

New international students in China usually learn three things in the first week: where the canteen is, how to top up a phone, and that the campus has more gates than expected. Almost nobody saves the emergency numbers, and the moment you need them is the moment you have no network, no Mandarin and no idea which service answers which situation.

The four numbers that cover almost everything

China runs separate numbers for police, fire, ambulance and traffic, and unlike the single 911 or 112 model, dialling the wrong one costs minutes. Police is 110, fire and rescue is 119, ambulance is 120 and traffic police is 122. All four are free to dial from any phone, including a phone with no SIM card.

Save them before you need them

Add all six to your phone as contacts, with the campus security number first. Write them on paper and keep it in your wallet, because a cracked screen or a dead battery is a realistic emergency scenario too.

What happens when you call 110

An operator answers in Mandarin and asks three things in order: where you are, what happened, and who you are. Have an address ready, not a landmark. Campus buildings have names and numbers, dormitories have block letters, and "near the big tree behind the library" will not get a car dispatched.

Language: what to expect

English-speaking operators exist in Beijing, Shanghai and a few other large cities, but coverage is not guaranteed. The reliable workaround is a three-way call: dial 110, say in Mandarin "qing deng yixia" (please hold), then conference in a Chinese-speaking friend, your class teacher or the campus security desk.

What not to do

Do not hang up after giving the address; stay on the line until the operator ends the call. Do not move an injured person before the ambulance arrives unless there is immediate danger. And do not assume a small theft is not worth reporting: the police record is the document your insurance claim depends on.

Campus security is faster than the police for campus incidents

The campus security office, usually called the bao wei chu, has guards on patrol inside the walls, knows the building layout and can be at your door in three minutes. For anything that happens on campus, call them first and let them decide whether the police are needed. They also handle lockouts, lost cards, noise complaints and escorts at night.

The 24-hour duty phone

Every university publishes a duty number that is answered around the clock, and it is printed on the back of your campus card in most cases. Photograph it on day one. The guard who answers can reach your class teacher at two in the morning, which is exactly what you need when a roommate stops breathing or a passport disappears.

Medical emergencies: 120, hospitals and insurance

An ambulance in China is a paid service, unlike the fire brigade. Expect a call-out fee plus mileage and any treatment given on board, typically a few hundred yuan in total, payable at the hospital or on the spot. If the patient can be moved safely by taxi to a nearby hospital, that is often faster than waiting for a dispatch.

What to bring to hospital

Bring your passport, your student card, your insurance card and a payment method. Chinese hospitals require registration and a deposit before treatment in most outpatient departments, and an international clinic inside a large hospital will handle English and direct billing with some insurers.

Keep every receipt

Insurance claims need four documents: the itemised bill, the diagnosis certificate, the receipt with the hospital's official stamp and, for accidents, the police report. Photograph all four before you leave the hospital, because replacing them means a return trip to the cashier's window.

Carrying ID is a legal requirement

Foreign nationals in China are required to carry their passport or residence permit at all times, and police can ask to see it. A photograph on your phone is not the same document. If you are stopped without it, you can be taken to the station for verification, which is an avoidable way to spend an evening.

The related rule most students break is address registration. Foreigners must register their residence with the local police station within 24 hours of moving, and the university files this for campus housing. If you move to a flat and the landlord does not file it, the fine falls on you, not on them.

After the emergency: the paperwork that pays you back

  • Get the police report number before you leave the station; the officer will write it on a slip.
  • Ask the hospital for an itemised bill, not just a total.
  • Keep the diagnosis certificate with the official red stamp.
  • Photograph your passport page, visa page and entry stamp for the insurer.
  • Tell the international office within 24 hours; they often need to notify the insurer themselves.
  • Keep the original documents until the claim is settled, not just scans.

Safety in China is generally high, and the numbers above are the reason the system works: a clear address and the right number get a response in minutes. The students who struggle are rarely in more danger than anyone else; they simply dialled 122 for a stolen phone, or called 110 and said "near the library".
