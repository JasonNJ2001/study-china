Scholarships in China for European Students: Government, Exchange and Department Awards

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-24

European students arrive at Chinese funding from an unusual position: most are eligible for three separate schemes at once, and almost none of them know it. The embassy, university and exchange routes have different deadlines, selectors and amounts, and applying to the wrong one is the commonest reason a strong candidate gets nothing.

The main scholarships for European students China administers fall into three families, and only one is the one everybody applies to. Knowing which fits your profile matters more than polishing a personal statement, because the selectors differ.

How the three funding routes differ

The Chinese Government Scholarship runs through an embassy quota, a university quota and a small ministry quota; provincial schemes are awarded by the province that funds the university; and Erasmus+ mobility is awarded by your European home institution under a partnership agreement. Each has its own application portal and its own deadline.

The embassy route is a quota, not a competition

Each Chinese embassy receives a fixed number of places for its host country and distributes them across candidates. That makes the competition national rather than global: you are compared with applicants from your own country, not with a stronger candidate from another continent. Small European countries with low application volumes sometimes have unfilled places.

The university route moves faster

A university with Type B places can nominate candidates directly, and its international office will say in one email whether places remain. Applying directly also means the same office handles your admission letter and JW202 form, removing the two-week lag embassy applicants face.

The provincial route is the least crowded

Provincial scholarships exist to internationalise a province's own universities, and most are worth 20,000 to 30,000 RMB a year against partial tuition. Because the university administers them rather than a national portal, applicants who ask the international office directly are often the only ones who apply.

What a Chinese Government Scholarship actually pays

A full Chinese Government Scholarship covers tuition, dormitory accommodation or a housing allowance, medical insurance of about 800 RMB a year, and a monthly stipend of 2,500 RMB for bachelor students, 3,000 RMB for master students and 3,500 RMB for doctoral students. The stipend is paid monthly into a Chinese bank account after registration.

Three details decide whether that total is enough:

  • Accommodation is a shared dormitory room; a single room usually costs an extra 300 to 800 RMB a month, paid by you.
  • Stipends are suspended, not cancelled, if you fail to register on time or take an unauthorised absence.
  • The scholarship does not cover flights, visa fees, the medical examination, or the residence permit fee of around 400 RMB.

Deadlines: the window is January to April

Almost every Chinese Government Scholarship route for a September intake opens in December or January and closes between March and April, with university programmes often closing first because they process nominations in batches. Embassy portals typically close in early April, and late applications are not carried forward to the following year.

What to have ready before the window opens

The documents that delay applications are never the essays. They are the notarised diploma, the notarised transcript, two academic reference letters on letterhead, the foreigner physical examination form completed within six months, a study plan, and a passport valid for another eighteen months. A police clearance certificate is required by some universities and not others.

Why January matters more than April

Embassies and universities award in rounds. A complete application filed in the first three weeks of January is read by a committee that still has all its places; the same file in late March competes for what is left. The quality gap between a January and an April applicant is smaller than the funding gap.

Erasmus+ and exchange agreements

Erasmus+ International Credit Mobility funds European students to spend three to twelve months at a Chinese partner university, with a monthly living grant and travel support, and it is awarded by your home university rather than by China. The money is smaller than a full Chinese Government Scholarship, but the selection is handled by people who already know you.

The catch is the partnership. Erasmus+ mobility only works where your university holds a signed agreement with a specific Chinese institution, so the Chinese university is chosen for you. If your department has no agreement, the bilateral and university routes are the only realistic paths.

What European applicants underestimate

Three requirements consistently derail otherwise competitive files, and all three are administrative rather than academic: language evidence that expires, a medical certificate older than six months, and a break in degree continuity that nobody explains. Each is fixable in a week if you know it exists, and unfixable at the deadline.

Language evidence

Chinese-taught programmes require HSK level 4 with 180 or above, and competitive ones ask for HSK 5. English-taught programmes require IELTS 6.0 or TOEFL 80, though some accept a medium-of-instruction letter. A scholarship can be withdrawn if the certificate expires before enrolment.

The medical examination

The foreigner physical examination form must be completed at a designated hospital and is valid for six months, which means an examination done in October is expired by the time an April decision arrives. Book it in February or March, not earlier.

Degree continuity

Doctoral applicants are expected to hold a master's degree in a related field, and gaps longer than two years raise questions a short paragraph will not answer. A one-page explanation, attached rather than embedded, is usually enough.

Building an application that survives review

Reviewers read hundreds of files, and most of them are eliminated on structure rather than merit: a missing notarisation, an oversized PDF, a reference letter that never arrived. Five habits change the outcome, and four of them take less than an hour to adopt before the portal closes.

  1. Apply to two routes in parallel: the embassy quota and one university programme, with different referees.
  2. Address the university's named supervisor or department in the study plan, because generic plans signal a generic application.
  3. Keep every document under the portal's size limit; oversized PDFs are rejected automatically.
  4. Ask the international office which provincial schemes it nominates for, and apply to those as well.
  5. Track each portal weekly from January; requests for supplementary documents arrive with a 72-hour window.

Funding a degree in China is mostly a scheduling problem disguised as a merit problem. The students who are funded are rarely the strongest in the pool; they filed a complete application in January, to a route with places left.
