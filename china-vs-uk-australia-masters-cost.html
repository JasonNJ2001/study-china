"China vs UK vs Australia: Cost of a Master's Degree"

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-10-26

Most cost comparisons fail for the same reason: they set one year of one country against a different number of years in another. A UK master's is usually twelve months. An Australian master's is typically two years. A Chinese master's runs two to three. Put their annual tuition side by side and you learn nothing about which will empty your account.

This comparison works the other way round: it starts with the whole degree, then spreads that total over three years so a one-year and a three-year programme sit on the same plane. The question behind any study in china vs uk cost debate is not which country is cheaper per year, but how much money leaves your family before you hold the certificate.

What the three systems actually charge for

They price education differently, and that matters more than the headline number.

In China, public university tuition is regulated. The government sets ceilings and universities publish international rates, so the gap between a provincial and a top-tier university is real but bounded. There is no domestic-versus-international split of the kind that exists in Britain.

In the UK, international fees are unregulated and set per programme. A master's is one intensive year, so you pay in twelve months what a Chinese university charges for four years.

In Australia, fees are set per programme too, but degrees are longer and the visa requires proof of living costs up front.

Living costs: rent decides the rest

Solve housing cheaply and you have solved two thirds of your budget.

China

Dormitories are the standard route and the reason Chinese totals stay low. A shared room costs roughly RMB 600 to 1,200 per month; a single room on campus, where available, RMB 1,200 to 2,500. Off-campus flats in a second-tier city start near RMB 1,800. Food, transport and phone add RMB 1,200 to 2,000 monthly.

United Kingdom

Halls outside London run GBP 500 to 750 monthly for an en-suite room; private renting is similar plus deposits and bills. London adds roughly 40 percent. Groceries and transport add GBP 300 to 450 monthly, so budget GBP 12,000 to 15,000 for the year outside London, GBP 16,000 to 20,000 inside it.

Australia

Student accommodation in Sydney or Melbourne runs AUD 350 to 550 per week, the most punishing line in this article. Regional universities cut that considerably. The visa requirement asks for roughly AUD 30,000 per year, and that is a floor, not a comfortable budget.

Costs each system buries

Charges that never reach the brochure:

  • China: medical insurance around RMB 800 per year, permit fees, and language training if your programme needs HSK 4 before entry.
  • China: dormitory deposits, plus return flights you will take more than once across a three-year programme.
  • UK: the Immigration Health Surcharge, payable up front for the whole visa, on top of the visa fee.
  • UK: a one-year programme leaves no room for a failed module. Resits and an extra term are expensive.
  • Australia: proof of funds covering the first year, and a rental deposit of typically four weeks' rent.
  • Australia: two years means two return flights and two years of insurance at Australian rates.

What scholarships actually change

Scholarships do not make an expensive country cheap. They make a specific programme affordable for a specific applicant.

  • Chinese Government Scholarship (CSC): tuition, dormitory and a monthly stipend. Competitive, with deadlines months before enrolment.
  • Provincial and municipal scholarships: less competitive than CSC, often partial, usually tied to a named university.
  • University-level waivers: full or partial tuition remission offered directly by the institution.
  • UK and Australian scholarships: real, but rarely cover living costs in full and skew towards research degrees.

A partial waiver in China can cut the total more than a full scholarship would in the UK, because the base cost is smaller. That is the arithmetic most applicants miss.

Where the cheap option turns out expensive

China is not automatically the rational choice.

English-taught programmes are uneven

English-taught master's degrees cluster around business, engineering, computer science and Chinese studies. For archaeology or musicology you may find nothing suitable, and a forced compromise costs more than a higher fee.

Work rights are narrower than they look

Legal part-time work in China exists but is capped and needs university plus exit-entry approval. The UK and Australia allow more hours, though neither lets a student earn a year's tuition.

The verdict

China wins on absolute cost by a wide margin, and nothing comes close at the provincial end. The UK wins on time: one year, one degree, no long exposure to rent. Australia sits last on cost in almost every configuration and is chosen for reasons other than money.

Decide by building your own version of the second table, with real programme fees and real rent for the city you would actually live in. Brochure prices usually mislead by several thousand dollars in one direction or the other.

If you would rather start from a shortlist than a spreadsheet, narrow the programmes first and price them second.
