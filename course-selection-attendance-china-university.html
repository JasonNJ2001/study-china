"Course Selection and Attendance Rules at Chinese Universities"

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-09

A Chinese degree is a credit ledger with hard rules attached, and the rules are enforced by a system most international students meet for the first time in their second week. Course selection opens for a few days, popular electives vanish in minutes, and attendance is recorded well enough to decide whether you sit the final exam at all.

How does course selection work at Chinese universities?

You select courses yourself through the university's academic affairs system (教务系统), usually in a window of three to seven days before each semester, and you register for both compulsory and elective courses. Compulsory courses are pre-loaded for your cohort; electives, physical education and general education modules are the ones you fight for, and they close fast.

The system assigns a class section and a teacher to each course, and some universities allocate popular sections by lottery rather than first-come. Check the timetable before confirming, because two electives that look fine separately can clash, and changes after the add-drop window need a paper form signed by both departments.

The first-week scramble

Elective places are released in stages, and a full section often reopens when someone drops. Refresh the system on the first day of teaching, attend the first class of anything you want, and ask the teacher whether they will sign an add form: a signature is worth more than a place in the queue.

What to check before you confirm

  • Credit value and whether it counts toward your major or as a free elective
  • Assessment method: exam, coursework or a mix
  • Language of instruction, which is not always stated in the English course list
  • Timetable clashes with compulsory labs
  • Whether the course runs for 16 weeks or only half a semester

Credits, contact hours and what a semester weighs

Chinese undergraduate degrees require roughly 140-170 credits over four years, and master's degrees 30-40 credits over two or three. One credit usually equals 16-18 contact hours in a semester, meaning a weekly 45-minute class for a full term, so a 4-credit course is four periods a week and a serious time commitment.

International students in English-taught programmes usually carry 20-25 credits per semester in the first year, which is heavier than it sounds because contact hours are only part of the load. Chinese universities count self-study, laboratory reports and group projects inside the credit, and the exam covers the textbook, not the lecture slides.

The course categories that confuse newcomers

Compulsory courses (必修课) are fixed for your cohort and cannot be swapped. Electives (选修课) are chosen. Public courses (公共课) cover physical education, Chinese language and, for Chinese students, ideological and political theory, which international students normally replace with Introduction to China (中国概况). Ask your international office which substitution applies to your programme.

Attendance rules: the two-thirds line

Most Chinese universities bar students from the final exam if absences exceed one third of the scheduled class hours, and the rule is written into the course assessment regulations rather than left to the teacher. At that point the course is recorded as failed regardless of coursework, and you retake it next year.

Attendance is taken by roll call, by signing a sheet passed around the room, or by a phone check-in inside a geofence. Sending a friend's signature is treated as serious misconduct, and teachers who notice report it. If you must miss class for illness or a visa appointment, submit the leave form in advance and keep the receipt.

What counts as an excused absence

Documented illness, official university activities, family bereavement and compulsory immigration appointments are normally excused with evidence. Travel, part-time work, a delayed flight and oversleeping are not, and a medical certificate from the campus clinic is worth more than one from a private clinic the office has never heard of.

Grading, GPA and the make-up exam

Most courses are graded on a 100-point scale with 60 as the pass mark, converted to a 4.0 or 5.0 GPA depending on the university. Scores below 60 mean a make-up exam (补考) before the next semester, and a second failure means retaking the course (重修) and paying a fee per credit.

GPA matters for scholarship renewal and for master's applications abroad, and Chinese transcripts list every attempt, so a retaken course often shows both scores. That is normal and explainable; a withdrawn semester is harder to explain.

Exams, coursework and the two-week exam period

Finals run in a designated two-week exam period at the end of each semester, in January and June or July. The timetable is published about a month ahead and clashes are resolved by the registry, not the teacher, so report a clash as soon as you see it rather than a week before the exam.

Many courses weight coursework at 30-40% and the final at 60-70%, and coursework means attendance, quizzes, a presentation and a report with a deadline that does not move. Chinese academic culture treats deadlines as fixed and plagiarism as a disciplinary matter, and a similarity check runs on every submission.

If you are ill during the exam period

Apply for a deferred exam (缓考) with a hospital certificate before the paper, not after. Deferred exams are usually scheduled with the make-up exams, and an unexplained absence is recorded as zero rather than as a deferral.

Where international students get caught out

The three recurring problems are credit load, silent failure and the assumption that attendance is optional. Students add electives to look impressive, discover that 28 credits with a language course is unmanageable, and drop one after the add-drop window, which leaves a fail on the transcript.

The second is silence. Chinese administrative systems do not chase students, so a missed deadline, an unregistered course or a failed make-up exam surfaces only when the transcript is printed. Check the academic affairs system every fortnight, and confirm your credit total with the international office before each semester ends.

A workable plan for your first semester

  1. Take the compulsory load plus one elective, not three, and confirm the total with your adviser.
  2. Attend every class for the first three weeks, which establishes you with teachers who sign add forms.
  3. Diarise the add-drop deadline, the exam timetable and every coursework submission in week one.
  4. Ask the international office in writing which public courses are substituted for your cohort.
  5. Check your transcript on the system at mid-term, not at the end of the year.

Students who do those five things rarely meet the two-thirds rule, the make-up exam or the academic warning. The system is rigid but legible: it publishes its rules, applies them uniformly, and rewards the student who reads them in September rather than in June.
