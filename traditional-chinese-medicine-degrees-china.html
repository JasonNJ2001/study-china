Traditional Chinese Medicine Degrees in China for International Students

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-19

Traditional Chinese Medicine looks like a short course in acupuncture from the outside and a five-year clinical degree from the inside. The difference decides where you can work afterwards, so it matters before you apply. This guide covers the structure, the HSK threshold, the hospital rotation, the cost and the licensing boundary for anyone planning to study TCM in China.

What a TCM degree in China actually is

Traditional Chinese Medicine is a five-year clinical bachelor's degree, not a certificate course in acupuncture. Students spend roughly three years on basic medical sciences and classical theory, then two years in supervised hospital rotation, and graduate with a Bachelor of Medicine. Tuition for international students typically falls between CNY 26,000 and 40,000 a year, and the default language of instruction is Chinese.

The degree follows the same national curriculum framework as clinical medicine, so graduates can sit postgraduate exams. It does not automatically give you the right to treat patients in China.

The five-year structure, year by year

Years one to three are campus-based: anatomy, physiology, diagnostics, herbal pharmacology, formulas, meridians and acupuncture points, plus a heavy load of classical texts read in Chinese. Year four moves into the teaching hospital for ward rounds in internal medicine, gynaecology, paediatrics and orthopaedics, usually in both TCM and Western departments. Year five is the internship year, and it is the part employers and licensing bodies ask about.

Chinese pharmacy is a separate four-year degree

Do not confuse 中医学 with 中药学. The pharmacy route runs four years, focuses on herbal identification, processing and quality control, and leads to laboratory, industry or regulatory work rather than patient contact. If your goal is clinical practice, you want the five-year 中医学 programme; if your goal is herbal product development, the four-year route is shorter and often cheaper.

Which universities teach TCM to international students

Around thirty Chinese institutions are authorised to enrol international students in TCM, and the strongest clinical programmes cluster in Beijing, Shanghai, Nanjing, Guangzhou and Chengdu. All of them appear on the Ministry of Education list of institutions permitted to take foreign students, which is the first thing to verify. Why the teaching hospital matters more than the ranking

A TCM degree is a clinical apprenticeship as much as a classroom qualification, so the affiliated hospital decides the quality of your fourth and fifth years. Look for separate TCM and Western internal medicine wards and a working acupuncture outpatient department. Ask admissions which hospital international students rotate through, and whether the rotation is observation-only or supervised hands-on.

Language requirements and the HSK threshold

The standard requirement for a Chinese-taught TCM bachelor's is HSK 4 with at least 180 in each section, and many clinical programmes now ask for HSK 5 at 180 or above because patient interviews demand fluent listening. Some universities accept HSK 4 for entry and require HSK 5 before the hospital year begins. Plan for the higher number, since the clinical years are unforgiving of weak listening.

If you have no Chinese, the realistic route is a one-year language programme at the same university, then entry into year one. Language-year students usually pay a lower tuition of CNY 12,000–18,000.

Classical Chinese is a separate hurdle

Modern HSK Chinese gets you through consultations but not through the textbooks: classical medical texts use a compact literary grammar that even native students find slow. Ask whether the programme runs a dedicated 医古文 support class for foreign students.

Clinical rotation and what patient contact looks like

The internship year places you in hospital departments under a supervising physician, and your role depends on your Chinese level and the hospital's policy. Students with strong Chinese take histories, assist with acupuncture and tuina sessions, and write up cases; students with weaker Chinese observe. Either way, the year is full-time, often with a night-shift rota, and assessed by department supervisors, not by examination.

Expect the rotation to be the hardest year socially: ward culture is hierarchical, notes go into a Chinese hospital system, and a supervisor who speaks no English is normal.

Licensing: what the degree does and does not allow

A Chinese TCM degree does not automatically let you practise in China. Clinical practice requires registration through the national physician licensing system, and for foreign graduates that path is narrow, city-dependent and usually requires a Chinese-language licensing examination plus employer sponsorship. Most international graduates either return home, where recognition of the degree varies by country, or move into research, herbal industry, education or wellness work.

Check your home country's recognition rules before you enrol, because they decide whether five years and CNY 150,000 or more produce a usable qualification. Some countries require a bridging exam; others accept the degree with credential evaluation.

Costs, scholarships and the application window

Beyond tuition, budget CNY 1,200–2,000 a month for dormitory accommodation and food in a provincial capital, and CNY 800–1,500 for textbooks and clinical supplies over the five years. The Chinese Government Scholarship covers tuition, dormitory and a monthly stipend, and its application window runs from January to April for the September intake, with undergraduate applicants required to be under 25 on 1 September of the entry year.

University-level and provincial scholarships for TCM are less competitive than CSC because fewer applicants target this field. Applying to two or three universities with different scholarship tracks in the same cycle is normal and costs only the application fees, typically CNY 400–800 each.

Questions to ask before you commit

Use this list in your first email to admissions, because the answers separate a well-run clinical programme from a marketing brochure. Specific answers name hospitals, HSK levels and graduate numbers; vague answers do not. Treat vagueness as a warning sign, since the sector has strong and weak programmes that look identical on a website.

  1. Which hospital will I rotate through in years four and five, and is the rotation observational or supervised hands-on?
  2. What HSK level must I hold before the clinical year starts, not just at entry?
  3. Is there a dedicated classical Chinese support class for international students?
  4. How many international students graduated from this programme in the last three years?
  5. Does the university provide any support for licensing or credential recognition in my home country?

Specific answers mean the programme is run properly; marketing language means keep looking, because five years is a long time on the wrong campus.
