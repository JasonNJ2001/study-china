Notarisation, Apostille and Certified Translation for Chinese University Applications

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-13

China has been a party to the Hague Apostille Convention since 7 November 2023, which means a degree certificate issued in another member state no longer needs a Chinese consulate stamp: one apostille from your own government is enough. Everything else about document preparation is unchanged. The notarisation must happen in the country that issued the document, and the translation must be certified.

Why Files Get Rejected Before Anyone Reads Them

Chinese admissions offices reject applications at the document-check stage rather than at academic review, and the usual cause is a notarisation performed in the wrong country. A Chinese notary cannot certify a foreign degree and a foreign notary cannot certify a Chinese one: the certificate has to be notarised in the jurisdiction that issued it, then authenticated for use in China.

This matters most for applicants who study in a third country and apply from there. A Nigerian degree notarised in Malaysia is not acceptable, even if the notary is impeccable, because the notary had no authority over the issuing institution.

The Apostille Change and What It Replaced

Before 7 November 2023 every foreign public document heading to China needed two separate authentications: notarisation at home, then legalisation by the Chinese embassy or consulate. China's accession to the Apostille Convention removed the second step for documents from member states, cutting roughly two to six weeks and several hundred dollars from a typical application.

Which countries are covered

Member states include the United Kingdom, the United States, Australia, Japan, South Korea, India, South Africa, Germany, France, Italy, Spain, the Netherlands, Brazil, Mexico and most of the European Union. Canada acceded in January 2024. If your country is not a member, the old route still applies: notarisation followed by consular legalisation, which routinely takes four to eight weeks.

What the apostille has to say

An apostille is a numbered square certificate attached to the notarised document by a competent authority: the Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office in the UK, a state Secretary of State in the United States, the Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade in Australia. It must be attached to the notarised original rather than to a loose photocopy, and it carries an issue date that Chinese universities read as part of the document chain.

What Chinese Universities Actually Ask For

Most universities want three documents notarised: the degree certificate, the full transcript, and the graduation certificate if it is issued separately. Chinese or English translations are accepted, the translation must be bound with the copy rather than stapled to it, and the notary's certificate must state that the translation corresponds to the original.

Certified Translation: The Rules That Catch People

Translation is where files quietly fail. Chinese universities accept Chinese or English, a translation must be certified by a notary or an official translation service, and self-translated documents are rejected outright even when they are accurate. Accuracy is not the test; provenance is.

Who can certify a translation

In most countries the notary who notarises the copy can certify the translation in the same appointment, which is the cheapest route. In the UK a solicitor or a translator holding an Institute of Translation and Interpreting stamp is accepted. In the United States a translator's signed certificate of accuracy is normally enough without notarisation, though a few Chinese universities still ask for the notary stamp on top.

Chinese-language translation and course names

If you translate into Chinese, course names matter more than the certificate wording. A transcript listing "Linear Algebra II" should map to a recognisable Chinese course title, because Chinese academics review transcripts by course name when deciding whether to award credit. Ask the translator to keep the original course code in brackets.

Costs, Turnaround and Common Mistakes

Budget $30–80 per document for notarisation and apostille in most countries, or 200–500 RMB per document if a Chinese notary handles a document already in Chinese. Turnaround is typically five to ten working days, two to four weeks for consular legalisation in non-member states, and same-day service at some US state offices and UK post offices.

  1. Notarising a photocopy of a photocopy, which produces a document that certifies nothing.
  2. Ordering a single copy, then discovering the university, the visa centre and the scholarship panel each want one.
  3. Translating only page one of a three-page transcript.
  4. Sending the apostille in a separate envelope, so the two arrive apart and both are refused.
  5. Assuming a scan is enough at enrolment when the university wants the paper original on registration day.

When to Start Document Work

Start as soon as you shortlist universities, well before the Chinese Government Scholarship window opens in January and closes in April. Notarisation and apostille take two weeks in a good month, consular legalisation in a non-member state can take eight, and that is before a courier loses the envelope or a registrar goes on leave.

A workable sequence is: order certified copies from your university registry first, because that is the slowest step and outside your control; then book the notary; then the apostille; then the translation, if the notary cannot certify it; and only then the online application. Applicants who reverse the order end up uploading an incomplete file to hit a deadline, which is worse than applying a month later with a complete one.
