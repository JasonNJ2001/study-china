Sending Money To and From China as a Student: Remittance and Exchange Rules

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-20

Money is the part of studying in China that nobody covers at orientation. You land with a foreign card, a Chinese account that will not open for two weeks, and a rulebook written for residents rather than visitors. None of it is hostile, but the resident versus non-resident distinction decides which counter you queue at.

The quota that governs almost everything

China applies an annual facilitation quota of USD 50,000 per person to foreign exchange purchases by residents, tracked against an identity number rather than a bank account. Foreign students are normally classified as non-residents, so outbound transfers are handled as non-resident remittances: passport, visa or residence permit, and proof of the source of funds, with no fixed annual ceiling but considerably more paperwork per transaction.

The practical consequence is that the famous USD 50,000 figure does not cap what a student can send home. It caps what a Chinese resident can convert without documentation. A student moving a stipend or family money abroad is on the non-resident path, where the bank asks not how much but where it came from.

Residents, non-residents and why the counter differs

A resident means a Chinese citizen with household registration, or a foreign national holding a permanent residence ID card. A student on an X1 visa with a residence permit stays a non-resident for foreign exchange purposes even after four years in the country. That classification decides whether the bank applies the quota automatically or opens a file.

Sending money out of China: three routes that work

Outbound transfers run through three practical channels: a SWIFT wire from your bank's app or counter, a UnionPay card used at an ATM abroad, and a mobile wallet linked to a Chinese account. Each carries a different cost profile and leaves a different paper trail, so the cheapest option depends on whether you are moving CNY 2,000 or CNY 40,000 at once.

SWIFT wires and the intermediary bank problem

A wire from Industrial and Commercial Bank of China or Bank of China to a European account usually passes through one or two correspondent banks, each deducting a fee before the money lands. Send CNY 3,000 and fixed costs can eat 4% of it; send CNY 40,000 and they become noise. Ask the clerk for the all-in cost, and give the receiving bank's SWIFT code plus the beneficiary name exactly as printed.

UnionPay card limits while travelling

UnionPay cards issued in China work at most overseas ATMs, but the limits are tight: about CNY 10,000 equivalent per card per day and CNY 100,000 per person per year, with a per-withdrawal cap set by the foreign bank. This is a travel tool, not a remittance channel. If you plan to send money from China abroad every term, an ATM will not carry it.

Receiving money from home

Inbound is easier than outbound. Money arrives either as a SWIFT transfer into your Chinese account or as a cash pickup through Western Union or MoneyGram, and the choice usually comes down to how fast you need it and whether the sender can read a SWIFT code correctly. Both routes require your name to match your passport exactly, including middle names and the order of characters.

Western Union and MoneyGram counters

Bank of China, Postal Savings Bank of China and China Construction Bank all operate agent counters for both networks. A pickup takes ten to thirty minutes after the sender's transaction clears, needs your passport and the ten-digit tracking number, and is capped per transaction by the sender's country rules. Fees are paid by the sender, which makes this the easiest option for a parent who does not want to fill in a wire form.

A direct transfer into your Chinese account

A SWIFT transfer needs the bank's English name, its SWIFT code, your account number and the branch name in pinyin. Money usually lands in one to three business days, and the receiving bank may call you to confirm the purpose of the payment. Tell them it is living expenses or tuition paid by a family member; mismatched narration is the most common reason a small transfer gets held for a week.

Paying tuition without triggering a review

Most universities accept tuition through the online portal, a bank transfer to the university's account, or a third-party payment platform. Paying through the portal is the safest route because the university's finance office reconciles it automatically and the receipt appears in your student record within days, which matters when you need it for a visa extension or a scholarship renewal.

If you pay by international wire, quote the student ID in the narration field and keep the SWIFT confirmation. Universities routinely hold unattributed transfers in a suspense account for weeks, and some charge a late fee after the second week of term.

Cash, customs and the USD 5,000 line

Travellers entering or leaving China with more than USD 5,000 in foreign currency, or more than CNY 20,000 in renminbi, must declare it on the customs form. Undeclared cash above the threshold can be confiscated, and repeat offences are treated as currency smuggling. For a student this rarely matters, but it matters enormously the first time a relative hands you an envelope before a flight.

Carry what you need for two weeks and leave the rest in a bank. Chinese campuses are close to cashless: canteens, printing shops and campus buses all take a card or a QR code.

A checklist before your first transfer

Run through these six items before you touch a transfer form. Each one is a documented reason a payment bounces back after three days of waiting, and each is fixed in minutes at the counter. Banks resolve these errors slowly, so the five minutes spent checking a name against a passport saves a week of interest-free limbo.

  1. Confirm your account is a personal RMB account opened with your passport, not a campus e-wallet.
  2. Write your name exactly as it appears on the passport photo page.
  3. Get the receiving bank's SWIFT code and branch name in English from the bank, not from a search engine.
  4. Ask the counter for the total cost including correspondent bank fees.
  5. Keep every receipt until the money is confirmed in the destination account.
  6. Check the central parity rate published by the People's Bank of China each trading morning before converting a large sum.

Exchange rates move a few tenths of a percent a day, which on CNY 50,000 is the price of a month of canteen meals. Convert in tranches rather than all at once, and never at an airport kiosk.
