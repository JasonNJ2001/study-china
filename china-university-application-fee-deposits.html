"Application Fees and Enrollment Deposits at Chinese Universities"

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-10

Applying to a Chinese university costs money before you have a visa, a room or a seat in a classroom, and the two payments that catch people out are the application fee and the enrollment deposit. Neither is large by international standards, but both are usually non-refundable at the moment you most want your money back.

How much is a China university application fee?

The China university application fee is usually RMB 400-800, roughly USD 55-115, charged once per application when you submit the online form. Tsinghua and several other top-ranked universities charge RMB 800, while provincial universities commonly ask RMB 400-600. The fee is not refundable if you are rejected or if you change your mind.

Some universities let you name a second programme on the same form for one fee. Others charge again, so read the payment page before you submit twice and pay twice for a decision you only needed once. The fee covers administrative review of your documents only, not an assessment of your chances.

Where the fee goes

Staff time, document verification and, at some universities, a preliminary academic review by the department. A fee of RMB 800 at a university receiving thousands of international applications a year is a filter, and it is designed to be one: it discourages speculative applications that are never completed.

What the fee does not include

Document courier, notarisation, CHSI or CSCSE verification of your previous qualifications, and the physical examination required at entry are all billed separately. Budget RMB 1,000-2,500 for that cluster, because the JW202 file and the residence permit application both depend on it.

The enrollment deposit: what you are actually paying

An enrollment deposit, often called a seat deposit or prepayment (占位费), is a partial tuition payment of roughly RMB 3,000-10,000 that a university asks for after you accept an offer. It is what unlocks the admission letter package and the JW202 form, and your student visa application cannot start until both documents are in hand.

The deposit is credited against your first year's tuition, so it is not an extra cost if you enrol. It becomes an extra cost only if you do not, which is precisely the mechanism: the university is holding a seat that it could have given to another applicant.

Scholarship holders normally skip the deposit entirely, because the funding body has already committed the tuition. If a university asks a CSC awardee for a deposit, ask the international office to confirm in writing which budget line pays it.

When your JW202 is issued

The JW202 form, formally the Visa Application for Study in China, is issued by the university only after the deposit clears and your documents pass review, typically five to fifteen working days after payment. The original is then couriered to you, and the courier fee of RMB 150-300 is charged to the applicant.

Nothing about the visa process starts before that paper arrives. Some Chinese embassies accept a scanned JW202 with a barcode in urgent cases, but most require the original, and a photocopy is not a substitute. Plan six to eight weeks between paying a deposit and holding a visa.

Why the form cannot be rushed

The JW202 is generated through a provincial education department system that reports your enrolment to the immigration authority. The university cannot issue it before your file is approved, and no amount of emailing the admissions officer changes that queue.

Refund rules: what you get back if plans change

Deposit refunds depend on how far your file has progressed, and the cutoff is almost always the JW202. Before the form is issued, most universities refund the deposit minus a handling fee; after it is issued, the deposit is normally forfeited, because the university has already reported your place to the education authority and must formally release it.

The application fee is never refunded at any stage. Some universities also deduct a registration or administrative charge of RMB 500-1,000 from a pre-JW202 refund, and that figure is stated in the offer letter, not on the website.

The three refund stages

  1. Before document review completes: full refund minus the application fee and bank charges.
  2. After admission but before JW202 issuance: deposit refunded minus a handling fee, on written request.
  3. After JW202 issuance: deposit forfeited, and releasing the place may require a formal withdrawal letter.

Get the refund rule in writing before you pay. An offer letter that is silent on refunds is a negotiation, not a contract, and email is the record that matters if a dispute appears months later.

Hidden costs around the application

Beyond the two headline payments, budget for notarised copies of your certificates and transcripts, verification through CHSI or CSCSE, a medical examination on a Chinese form, and passport photographs to the exact specification. Together these add RMB 1,000-2,500 and several weeks of calendar time.

None of them are optional. The JW202 file expects the medical form, the residence permit application expects the verified documents, and every gap surfaces as a delay at the visa counter rather than at admission, which is the worst possible moment to discover it.

How to pay safely from abroad

Pay by international bank transfer to the university account printed on the offer letter, or through the payment gateway inside the official application portal. Chinese universities publish their account details on the international students page, and a request to send money to a personal account, a friend's account or a payment app is fraud.

Bank charges on an international transfer are typically RMB 100-250 at each end, and the receiving bank may deduct a further fee, so the amount that arrives can be slightly short. Send the exact figure plus a margin, and keep the SWIFT confirmation, because the admissions office will ask for it.

A checklist before you transfer money

  1. Confirm the account name matches the university's legal name, not a department or a person.
  2. Ask in writing whether the deposit is refundable before JW202 issuance.
  3. Check whether the fee covers one programme or several.
  4. Keep the SWIFT or gateway receipt as a PDF, named clearly.
  5. Ask which email address confirms receipt, and wait for that confirmation before booking anything.
  6. Never pay an agent a deposit on the university's behalf without a receipt issued by the university.

Two payments, both modest, both irreversible at the wrong moment. Treat them as the first administrative test of the whole degree: read the fine print, keep the receipts, and confirm every step in writing. The students who plan this stage calmly are the ones who are not surprised by a forfeited deposit in August.
