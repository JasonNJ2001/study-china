Medical Care in China for International Students: Hospitals, Costs and Emergencies

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-05

Medical care in China for an international student is provided by a graded public hospital system, not by a family doctor. You choose the tier yourself, pay at the counter, and claim afterwards from the compulsory insurance bought at enrolment. A routine consultation costs RMB 200 to 600 at a top public hospital and five times that at a private international clinic.

How Chinese hospitals are graded

Public hospitals carry a three-tier grade, and the top tier, Grade 3 Class A, is where complex cases belong. Each tier has an outpatient hall where you register, pay and queue on the same day, and there is no referral system to navigate: you can walk into a Grade 3A hospital in any city without a letter from anyone. The trade-off is waiting time.

The international departments

Most Grade 3A hospitals in major cities run an international medical department or a foreigner clinic, with English-speaking staff, appointment booking and shorter queues at three to ten times the price. They are worth it for anything involving explanation and consent, such as a specialist consultation or a procedure, and unnecessary for a blood test or a prescription refill.

What a visit actually costs

A standard outpatient visit at a Grade 3A public hospital costs RMB 200 to 600 including registration, consultation and basic blood work, and the registration fee alone is RMB 10 to 50 for a general slot. Specialist slots cost more and sell out early. Inpatient care and surgery are where the gap between public and private becomes a different order of magnitude.

Prices are set by provincial health commissions rather than by the hospital, which is why a scan costs roughly the same in Chengdu as in Wuhan and considerably less than in Beijing.

The insurance you are required to buy

Nearly every university requires international students to hold the Comprehensive Insurance for International Students in China, a group policy sold through Chinese insurers and priced at roughly RMB 400 to 800 a year. It covers hospitalisation up to a stated annual limit, part of outpatient costs after a deductible, and accident treatment, and it is checked at registration each September.

What CSC students get instead

Chinese Government Scholarship students receive medical insurance as part of the award, arranged by the China Scholarship Council and valid for the scholarship period. It is not the same product as the commercial policy, and it typically excludes dental care, optical care, cosmetic treatment and pre-existing conditions, so read the schedule before assuming you are covered.

The claim process nobody explains

Keep every receipt, the diagnosis note and the itemised bill, because reimbursement requires all three and the hospital will not reissue them later. Claims are usually filed through the university's international office within a set window, often 30 to 90 days, and paid into a Chinese bank account, which is one more reason to open one in your first week.

Emergencies and the numbers to know

Ambulance is 120, police is 110 and fire is 119, and all three are free to dial from any phone including a foreign SIM in roaming. Ambulance dispatch is handled in Chinese, so the practical advice is to hand the phone to a Chinese speaker, or to take a taxi to the nearest Grade 3A emergency department, which is often faster in city traffic.

  • Say the district and the nearest landmark, not the street address
  • State the age, sex and the main symptom in one sentence
  • Do not move someone with a suspected spinal injury
  • Bring your passport and insurance card, or a photo of both
  • Expect to pay a deposit at the emergency desk before treatment continues

What the emergency department will ask for

Payment first, in most public hospitals, for anyone without Chinese public insurance. The deposit is typically RMB 2,000 to 5,000 for an emergency admission and is settled against the final bill. Card machines accept UnionPay reliably and foreign Visa or Mastercard unpredictably, so carry a Chinese bank card or enough cash for the first night.

Paying as a foreigner

Chinese hospitals are effectively cashless for locals and awkward for visitors. Registration, tests and pharmacy all take WeChat Pay or Alipay, both of which require a Chinese bank account or an international card linked to the app. Foreign cards fail often enough that the reliable combination is a Chinese debit card, some cash, and a friend who can pay and be reimbursed.

Opening the account before you need it

A student bank account takes about an hour at a branch with your passport, residence permit and admission letter, and it is the single most useful piece of admin in your first month. Without it you cannot use mobile payment, receive an insurance reimbursement, or pay a hospital deposit at three in the morning.

Dental, optical and the exclusions

Student insurance excludes dental treatment, spectacles, contact lenses, vaccination for travel, and anything classified as cosmetic. Dental care is also expensive in absolute terms: a filling costs RMB 300 to 800, a root canal RMB 1,500 to 3,000, and an implant RMB 8,000 to 15,000, all payable in full at the chair. Get dental work done before you leave home.

What to do in your first week

Register at the campus clinic, photograph your insurance card and policy number, save 120 in your phone, and locate the nearest Grade 3A hospital on a map. Ask the international office which hospital your insurance lists as a direct-billing partner, because paying upfront and claiming later is workable but slow, and direct billing removes the deposit problem entirely.
