How Much Does It Cost to Study in China Per Year

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-10-22

Most published figures for studying in China are either a scholarship brochure number or an agent quote with a commission folded into it. Neither helps you plan a real budget. The practical answer is that one academic year lands somewhere between 45,000 and 180,000 RMB, and which end you reach is decided by four things you actually control: city, subject, housing type, and degree level.

Below is what each line item costs, and where the surprises hide.

Tuition: the line that varies most

Tuition is quoted per academic year, not per semester, at almost every Chinese university. The range is wide enough that the same word, "bachelor's degree," can mean 16,000 RMB or 50,000 RMB depending on what you study.

  • Language year or non-degree study: 12,000 to 22,000 RMB
  • Bachelor's in humanities, business or law: 16,000 to 30,000 RMB
  • Bachelor's in engineering or hard sciences: 20,000 to 35,000 RMB
  • Clinical medicine (MBBS): 30,000 to 50,000 RMB
  • Master's, taught: 22,000 to 45,000 RMB
  • MBA or professional master's: 40,000 to 90,000 RMB

Public versus private providers

Public universities, which means nearly every university you have heard of, sit in the ranges above. Independent colleges and Sino-foreign joint programmes charge two to three times more for the same degree title, and they advertise in English far more aggressively, which is why applicants often assume the expensive figure is the normal one. It is not.

Why subject beats ranking

Ranking barely moves the price. A top-ten comprehensive university in Wuhan or Nanjing can charge less than a mid-tier private college in Shanghai. What moves the price is cost of delivery: a medicine degree needs clinical placements, an engineering degree needs lab time, an MBA needs case-study faculty. If your budget is the binding constraint, the subject you choose matters more than the university's position in a league table.

Housing: dormitory or apartment

Nearly all universities require first-year international students to live on campus. After that the choice opens up, and it is the second largest line in your budget.

On-campus dormitory rates

  • Four-person shared room: 3,000 to 6,000 RMB per year
  • Two-person room: 6,000 to 12,000 RMB per year
  • Single room: 12,000 to 20,000 RMB per year

Dormitory rates are per bed, per year, and they include utilities and internet at most institutions. They are heavily subsidised, so a campus single often costs less than a shared flat off campus.

Off-campus rent and the deposit trap

In a second-tier city, a one-bedroom apartment runs 1,500 to 3,000 RMB per month. In Beijing or Shanghai, budget 4,000 to 7,000 RMB. The monthly rent is not the real number, though. You will usually pay a deposit of one to three months plus an agency fee that is commonly half of one month's rent, all upfront, in cash, before you have a bank account that works properly. Plan for three months of rent as a move-in cost.

Living costs by city tier

This is the variable applicants underestimate most.

  • Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Shenzhen: 2,000 to 3,500 RMB per month
  • Second-tier cities such as Nanjing, Hangzhou, Wuhan, Chengdu, Xi'an: 1,500 to 2,500 RMB per month
  • Smaller provincial cities: 1,000 to 1,800 RMB per month

A canteen meal costs 10 to 20 RMB. A delivery-app meal costs 25 to 40 RMB. That single habit, repeated three times a day, is worth roughly 20,000 RMB a year, more than most applicants spend choosing a university.

Fixed costs that get left out

These are small individually and routinely absent from agent quotes:

  • Comprehensive medical insurance: 600 to 1,000 RMB per year, mandatory, purchased through the university
  • Residence permit: 400 RMB for under one year, 800 RMB for one to three years
  • Physical examination: 300 to 600 RMB, first year only
  • Books and materials: 500 to 1,000 RMB per year
  • Student visa fee at a Chinese embassy: roughly 400 to 1,000 RMB depending on nationality
  • Phone plan: 50 to 100 RMB per month
  • Flights home: 4,000 to 9,000 RMB round trip, the line almost everyone forgets

How to lower the total without lowering the quality

  1. Apply for the CSC scholarship, a provincial scholarship and a university-level scholarship in the same cycle. They are separate systems and you can hold only one, but applying to all three costs nothing.
  2. Choose a strong public university in a second-tier city over a private college in a first-tier one. The tuition gap alone covers two years of living costs.
  3. Take the shared dormitory for year one, then move off campus in year two with people you have actually met.
  4. Eat on campus. This is the highest-leverage decision in the entire budget.
  5. Buy insurance through the university rather than a third-party broker. It is the same policy at a lower price.

What a scholarship actually changes

The Chinese Government Scholarship covers tuition, dormitory accommodation and a monthly stipend of 2,500 RMB for bachelor's and master's students, or 3,500 RMB for doctoral students. It does not cover flights, visa fees or the deposit on an apartment. Provincial and university scholarships are usually partial: a 25, 50 or 100 percent tuition waiver with no stipend at all.

So a fully funded year still leaves you roughly 15,000 to 25,000 RMB of incidental costs in a second-tier city. Budget for that even if you expect to win funding.

The number to plan around

If you are self-funding and want a defensible figure rather than a best case, plan 60,000 to 70,000 RMB per year for a bachelor's degree in a second-tier city, living in a shared or two-person dormitory and eating mostly on campus. That is about 8,500 to 10,000 USD. Everything above that is a choice, not a requirement.

Work out the annual cost to study in China honestly, add ten percent for the first year's setup costs, and you will be closer to reality than most of the numbers you will find advertised.
