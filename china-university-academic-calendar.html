"The Chinese University Academic Calendar: Semesters, Holidays and Exam Weeks"

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-08

Chinese universities do not run on the Western September-to-May rhythm, and the mismatch costs students money in airfares and marks in missed deadlines. The year starts in early September, peaks twice with exam weeks, and ends in early July, with holiday blocks that are generous but fixed by the State Council rather than by your school.

When does the Chinese academic year start and end?

The academic year begins in early September and finishes in early July, with the exact dates set by each university. Registration and orientation usually occupy the last week of August or the first days of September, teaching starts in the second week, and the year closes with graduation ceremonies in late June and degree certificates issued in July.

That fixed frame matters when you book flights. Arriving on the first day of teaching means missing orientation, course selection and the medical checks that must be completed before the residence permit application, so plan to land one to two weeks before the stated start date.

How is the year split into semesters?

Two semesters, each around 18 to 20 teaching weeks, separated by a winter break that spans Spring Festival. The autumn semester runs from September to mid or late January, the spring semester from late February to early July, and the split is deliberate: it places the long holiday where the lunar new year falls rather than at the calendar year boundary.

Autumn semester

Teaching runs from early September to late December, followed by two to three weeks of revision and examinations in January. Most first-year students take the heaviest credit load here, and the autumn semester is also when scholarship students complete their registration and annual review paperwork.

Spring semester

The spring semester starts after Spring Festival, usually in the third week of February, and runs to the end of June. Course selection happens in the first week, which is why arriving late costs you the popular electives rather than the compulsory modules.

How make-up workdays work

The catch is 调休, the make-up workday system: when a holiday falls near a weekend, the government shifts days so that a Saturday or Sunday becomes a working day. Since 2025 the statutory total is 13 days, with Chinese New Year's Eve and 2 May added as public holidays, and the published arrangement each November governs the following year.

Spring Festival travel

Spring Festival triggers the largest annual human migration on earth, and transport in the week before and after it is effectively sold out. Students who need to travel during that window should book rail tickets the moment sales open, roughly 15 days in advance, or stay on campus and accept that the city empties out.

What happens during exam weeks?

Examinations are concentrated in two blocks, mid to late January and late June, and results are published through the university portal within two to three weeks. A failed course is normally retaken in a resit period at the start of the next semester, in early March or early September, and the resit score is often capped at a pass.

Attendance rules are enforced more strictly than many international students expect. Missing more than a third of scheduled classes in a course can bar you from the examination regardless of your coursework, and that rule bites hardest in the weeks around National Day and Spring Festival when travel is cheap.

How long are the winter and summer breaks?

Winter break lasts four to six weeks and summer break runs about eight weeks, from early July to late August. Neither is a holiday for everyone: postgraduate students on research programmes continue in the laboratory, and language students often use the summer for an HSK attempt before the September intake deadlines.

Dormitories may close or consolidate during the winter break, and heating in northern cities is switched off in mid-March rather than at the end of the semester. Confirm with your building manager before you leave for a month, because a closed dormitory with your belongings inside is a problem nobody solves quickly.

What do scholarship students need to know about the calendar?

Scholarship students have calendar obligations that ordinary students do not, starting with the annual review that decides whether funding continues. The China Scholarship Council review is conducted between April and June, based on your transcript and a supervisor report, and a failed review can suspend the stipend for the following year.

Stipends are paid monthly and normally require physical presence in China, with deductions for absence longer than a month unless approved in advance. Chinese Government Scholarship students also complete a registration step each semester at the international office; missing it can pause payments even when your attendance is perfect.

How should you plan travel around the calendar?

Travel around the two exam blocks rather than the holidays, because a cheap flight is worthless if it lands in the middle of a resit. The most reliable windows are the first two weeks of the winter break, late June after examinations, and the National Day week in October, when the whole country travels and prices peak accordingly.

Bookings that need a calendar reminder

  1. Rail tickets, released about 15 days before departure and gone within minutes before Spring Festival.
  2. Return flights, cheapest 8 to 12 weeks before the September and February semester starts.
  3. Dormitory check-in dates, which open a week before registration and close early in some buildings.
  4. The April scholarship review, which needs a printed and stamped transcript first.
  5. Resit registration, which opens only in the first week of March and September.

Two dates deserve a calendar entry now: the January exam block and the June one. Around them, everything else, from Spring Festival rail tickets to the April scholarship review, falls into a rhythm you can plan a year ahead instead of discovering semester by semester.
