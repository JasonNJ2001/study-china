Switching Majors at a Chinese University: Windows, Rules and Real Risks

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-15

Changing major is possible at most Chinese universities, but it is an administrative process with a short window, a ranking cut-off and a visa consequence, not a conversation with an adviser. Students who assume it works like a Western add-drop period lose a year. Students who learn the three rules below in first semester usually get the move they want.

What Changing Major Means in the Chinese System

Three different mechanisms are translated as "change of major", and only one is a formal transfer. 转专业 is a competitive transfer into another school's programme; 大类分流 is the declaration of a major inside a broad enrolment category you were already admitted to; a minor or second degree adds a subject without moving you. Confusing them wastes the one window you have.

The formal transfer changes your student record, your programme code and, for international students, the major printed on your residence permit. The declaration inside a broad category usually does not, which is why applicants should check whether their offer is a named major or a category before they accept it.

The Official Window and How Short It Is

Applications are normally accepted once, at the end of the first academic year, with a second chance at the end of year two at some universities. The paperwork opens in April or May and closes within two to three weeks, and decisions are published before the September registration. Missing the window means waiting a full year.

Nothing about the timing is negotiable, and the notice is published on the school's own website rather than emailed to students. Check the academic affairs office page monthly from March, and ask the international student office in writing whether the same calendar applies to your cohort.

Academic Conditions You Have to Meet

Two filters decide most applications: a class ranking in your current major, usually the top 10 to 20 per cent, and a quota in the receiving department, typically 5 to 10 per cent of that department's intake. A department that is already full will not open a place regardless of your grades.

The receiving school also sets an assessment, which can be a written paper in the target subject, an interview, or both. Applicants from a different discipline are often asked to sit a make-up examination in the foundation courses they never took, and failing it ends the transfer even after the ranking filter is passed.

Foundation courses and year loss

Transferring between unrelated fields almost always costs time. Credits from the first year are mapped course by course, and anything not recognised has to be retaken, which can add a semester or a year. A transfer inside the same faculty usually maps most credits; a transfer from engineering to literature maps almost none.

Extra Steps for International Students

For international students the transfer also touches immigration paperwork. The major is recorded on the admission letter, on the JW202 form and on the residence permit, so a successful transfer requires updated documents from the university and a visit to the exit-entry administration to amend the permit.

Under the Exit and Entry Administration Law, changes to the details of a residence permit, including the field of study, must be reported within ten days. Universities usually handle this in a batch, but the student is the one who carries the passport, so confirm in writing that the amendment has been filed before the semester starts.

Documents to collect in advance

Ask for four documents at the moment the transfer is approved: the new admission letter, a revised JW202, a school certificate stating the change, and a transcript of recognised credits. Keep scanned copies, because the residence permit appointment and any later scholarship application will ask for them again.

How a Scholarship Reacts to a Major Change

Scholarship holders face a third approval layer, and this is where transfers quietly fail. Chinese Government Scholarship students normally cannot change major or institution in the first year, and later changes require the consent of the dispatching authority and the scholarship council before the university will process them.

Provincial and university scholarships vary. Some permit a change with written approval from the funding office; others suspend the award and require a fresh review. Never submit a transfer application without first asking the scholarship office in writing, because an approved transfer that voids the award is a worse outcome than staying.

Alternatives When the Transfer Is Refused

A refusal is not the end of the subject. Most universities allow a minor (辅修) from the second year, a second degree (双学位) for strong students, and free elective credits that can be steered towards the field you wanted. None of these changes your student record, and none affects the visa.

Minors and electives

A minor typically requires 20 to 30 credits taken alongside the main programme, taught in the evening or at weekends, with a certificate issued on completion. Electives are cheaper: choose the target department's foundation courses and you graduate with a transcript that shows the subject, which is what a graduate admissions committee actually reads.

Graduate entry as the real switch

For many students the efficient route is to finish the current bachelor's and switch at master's level, where admission is by application rather than by internal quota. A strong final-year project in the target field, plus two recommendation letters from that department's staff, is a realistic path that does not risk the visa or the scholarship.

Comparing the Four Routes

Each route has a different cost in time, risk and paperwork, and only the first one moves your student record. Deciding Before You Apply

Weigh the cost honestly before you file, because the process rewards certainty and punishes a change of mind halfway through: an approved transfer abandoned in week three still leaves a record and a permit amendment. Four questions settle most cases, and each has a factual answer you can obtain in a week.

  1. Which foundation courses will not be recognised, and how many extra semesters does that mean?
  2. What is the published quota in the receiving department this year?
  3. Does your scholarship permit a change, and who signs the approval?
  4. Does the target department admit international students at all, in the language you can study in?

If two of the four answers are unfavourable, the minor route is usually the better trade. It keeps the scholarship, the visa and the graduation date intact, and it produces a transcript that says what you actually studied.
