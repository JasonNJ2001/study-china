Emailing Professors and Using Office Hours at Chinese Universities

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-26

Most international students in China lose marks, references and research places not through bad work but through unanswered email. The rules of academic contact here are learnable and fairly consistent: a formal register, short written requests, a preference for scheduled meetings over drop-ins, and a clear line between the professional channel and the messaging app. Getting these right is worth more than another ten hours in the library.

Chinese faculty read email, on a different rhythm

University teachers in China check institutional mail reliably but not continuously, and a reply within one to three working days is normal rather than slow. Nobody is obliged to answer at weekends, and during the Spring Festival holiday in January or February a two-week silence is expected. Plan requests around that calendar instead of resenting it.

The 48-hour rule of thumb

If a request is time-sensitive, send it at least 48 hours before the deadline you care about, and say in the first line when you need the answer. A message that demands a same-day reply from a professor teaching four courses is read as presumptuous, and it is more likely to be deferred than prioritised. Urgency has to be justified, not asserted.

Why the institutional address matters

Write from your university account, not a personal Gmail or a messaging app. Chinese faculty filter aggressively, and a message from an unfamiliar address with an attachment is a plausible phishing attempt. Your student address carries your name, department and student number, which is exactly the identity signal that gets a message opened.

The subject line carries most of the weight

A Chinese academic inbox is sorted by subject line before anything is opened, so the subject should contain the course code, your name and the action you want. Something like "HIST201 — Li Wei — request for meeting on essay topic" is processed in two seconds. "Question" or "Hello professor" is not, and it is often archived unread because it looks like spam or a mass mailing.

What to put in the first two lines

State who you are — name, student number, course and class section — then the single thing you want. Faculty here often teach several hundred students, and anonymity is the main reason a reasonable request is ignored. Two lines of identification cost you nothing and remove the only excuse for not replying.

Attachments and file names

Attach PDF rather than DOCX, because the formatting survives on any machine and the document cannot be edited in transit. Name the file with your surname and the purpose: "Zhang-research-proposal.pdf". Attachments named "document1" or in an unfamiliar format are frequently not opened at all.

How to address a professor without getting it wrong

The safest form is the title plus surname, and the trap is translating the Chinese habit literally. Students in China say 老师 (laoshi), which means teacher, and the direct English rendering "Teacher Wang" sounds wrong to any academic ear. Office hours exist, but you usually have to ask

The fixed weekly office hour is a Western convention that Chinese universities have adopted unevenly. Many departments publish 答疑时间 (question-and-answer time) in the course outline; others expect you to approach the lecturer after class or to request an appointment. The absence of a published hour does not mean access is closed, only that access is arranged.

Asking for an appointment

Request a specific slot rather than asking whether the professor is free: "Could I meet for fifteen minutes on Thursday afternoon or Friday morning?" A concrete proposal is accepted far more often than an open question, because it requires one decision instead of a search through a calendar. Confirm the room in the reply, since departmental offices move.

Preparing for a short meeting

Chinese faculty appointments are typically short and agenda-driven. Bring the draft, the specific question and the decision you need, in that order. A meeting that opens with background and reaches the question at minute twelve wastes the slot, and the professor will end it politely and without the answer you came for.

What belongs in WeChat and what does not

Many supervisors and class teachers will add you on WeChat, and this is normal rather than intimate. What follows is a professional channel with informal manners: short messages, no voice notes, no messages after 22:00, and no documents that matter sent only there. WeChat is for scheduling; email is for anything you may later need to prove.

The boundary that protects you

Keep every substantive request in email, even if you also mention it in WeChat. Chinese universities resolve disputes about supervision, deadlines and approvals by producing written records, and a chat thread that has been deleted on one phone proves nothing. A one-line email after the chat — "confirming what we agreed" — costs thirty seconds and settles arguments months later.

A template that gets a reply

The structure below works for almost any academic request in China, and it fits in under 150 words. The order matters: identity, request, reason, deadline, thanks. Follow it literally for the first few months, because the sequence is what makes the message readable to a professor skimming forty others on a Monday morning, and once you see replies arriving you can vary the wording.

  1. Subject: course code, your name, the action requested.
  2. First line: name, student number, programme and class.
  3. Second line: the single thing you are asking for, in one sentence.
  4. Third: one sentence of context, not a paragraph.
  5. Fourth: the date by which you need an answer, phrased as information.
  6. Attachment, if any, named with your surname and PDF format.
  7. Closing with your full name and student number again.

When silence means something

Two follow-ups a week apart are appropriate; a third within the same week is not. If there is still no answer, change channel: ask the teaching assistant, the class monitor, or the department office, and mention that you wrote twice. Silence from a professor is usually overload rather than refusal, and the department office can often resolve in a day what email could not in a fortnight.
