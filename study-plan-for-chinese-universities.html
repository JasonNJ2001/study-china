"How to Write a Study Plan for Chinese Universities: Structure and Scoring"

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-10-28

Most applicants write the study plan last, in a single evening, from a template downloaded somewhere. Then the rejection arrives and they assume the grades were not good enough. Usually the grades were fine. The plan was the problem, and nobody told them what it was being measured against.

A study plan for china scholarship review is not a personal statement and not a research proposal. It sits between the two, and reviewers read it for evidence rather than for style. This guide gives the structure that survives that reading and names the mistakes that lose points without the applicant ever finding out.

What the document is actually for

Who reads it, and for how long

A scholarship panel at a large Chinese university may process several hundred files in a review cycle. Your plan gets a first read of two to four minutes. If it passes, a second reader spends longer on it and cross-checks your claims against your transcripts, your two recommendation letters and your language certificate.

That first read is a filter. It looks for reasons to move on, and long paragraphs with no visible structure supply those reasons for free.

The three questions behind the score

Every reader is answering the same three questions, in this order:

  1. Can this person handle the coursework in the language it is taught in?
  2. Does this specific programme match them, or would any university in any country have done?
  3. Will they graduate and use the degree, or drift?

A plan that answers all three is competitive even if the writing is plain.

A section-by-section template

Five sections. Nothing else. Total length one and a half to two pages, roughly 800 to 1,000 words in English, or 1,200 to 1,500 characters in Chinese if the programme requires Chinese.

1. Academic background and the gap you want to close

State your degree, your institution, your graduation year and your grades in the system that country uses. Then name one specific limitation in your training. Not a vague weakness, a concrete gap.

Weak: "I want to deepen my knowledge and broaden my horizons."

Strong: "My undergraduate programme covered power electronics but had no laboratory component in grid integration, which is the part of the field I now need."

2. Why this programme, at this university

Name the programme, the department and two or three courses or laboratories that exist there and nowhere else in your shortlist. Name a supervisor only if you have read their recent work and can say what it connects to.

A reader can tell within one sentence whether you researched the programme or pasted a paragraph that would fit any university in the country. Leftover names from an earlier draft are the single most damaging error in this section.

3. The study plan itself

This is the part most applicants compress and should not. Give a year-by-year breakdown: coursework in year one, laboratory or fieldwork in year two, thesis in the final year. If the programme has a fixed curriculum, describe the sequence you expect to follow and which electives you intend to take.

If a thesis is required, propose a topic in one sentence and give two possible research questions. You are not committing to them. You are showing that you know what research in this field looks like.

4. Plan after graduation

Say what you will do, where, and for whom. "Return to my home country and work in the energy ministry" is acceptable. "Become a bridge between China and my country" is not, because it means nothing and reviewers have read it a thousand times.

If your plan involves returning to an employer, name the employer. If it involves a PhD, name the field. Specificity here is read as seriousness.

5. Why China

Short. Two or three sentences. Connect the country to the field, not to the scenery. A student in transportation engineering has a real reason to study high-speed rail in China. A student who writes about "ancient culture and rapid development" has given the reader nothing.

Mistakes that cost points quietly

  • Naming a supervisor who has retired or moved to another university.
  • Describing the programme with the wrong degree title, which signals a copy-paste without a final read.
  • Listing courses you want to take without saying what you would do with them.
  • Claiming a publication, competition result or internship that no document in your file supports.
  • Omitting any timeline at all, which makes the plan untestable and therefore unimpressive.
  • Exceeding two pages. Reviewers do not read past the limit and neither will you get credit for the extra text.
  • Submitting machine-translated text. It reads as fluent and hollow, and it is spotted immediately.

Formatting and language rules that are not optional

  1. Use the university's own form if one exists, even if it is ugly. A custom-designed PDF on a programme that supplied a template reads as not following instructions.
  2. Put your name, passport number and programme name in the header of every page.
  3. Use plain sentences. Short paragraphs, one idea each, no rhetorical questions.
  4. Write in the language of instruction. If the programme is taught in Chinese and you hold HSK 4, write in Chinese and let a native speaker correct it.
  5. Export to PDF, check that fonts embedded correctly, and open the file on a phone before uploading.

Adapting it for a CSC application

The Chinese Government Scholarship form asks for a study plan in a fixed box with a character limit, and the same text is forwarded to your chosen universities. Write the version for the university first, then cut it down. Do not write a generic version and send it to three institutions.

CSC reviewers weight the plan against the priority of your field and the fit with your chosen university. If your field appears on the current priority list, say so in the first paragraph rather than hoping the reader notices. If your first-choice university has already issued an acceptance letter or pre-admission notice, mention it in the opening lines, because that changes how the rest of the file is read.

A final check before submitting

Read the plan once as a stranger who has your transcript in front of them. Every claim should be traceable to a document in the file. Every programme detail should be checkable on the university website. Every sentence about the future should name a place, a field or an employer.

If a sentence could be pasted into anyone else's application, delete it. What remains is your study plan, and it will be shorter and considerably stronger than the version you started with.
