Is China Safe for International Students? What Europe's October Unrest Actually Reveals

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-10-20

Is China Safe for International Students? What Europe's October Unrest Actually Reveals

On Saturday 17 October, around 550,000 people took to the streets across France. By the French Interior Ministry's count, 2,397 were arrested and 997 police officers and gendarmes were injured, seven seriously. In Lyon a tourism office building was set on fire and looted; in Limoges the town hall burned; in Marseille and Lille demonstrators broke into municipal buildings. Near Bologna's central station, clashes injured more than 30 law enforcement officers. In Liège, 45 people were detained. The same weekend brought housing protests across Lisbon, Porto and Coimbra, and student demonstrations in Brussels.

Read that paragraph twice if you are a family choosing where a child will spend four years. But not for the reason it first appears.

The headline is the least useful part of the story

Nothing above means Europe is a bad place to study. University cities in France, Italy, Belgium and Portugal remain safe by any reasonable standard, the violence was concentrated in city centres over roughly two days, and it was condemned across the political spectrum. Almost no international student was anywhere near it.

What the weekend did reveal is something that almost never makes it into a study-abroad comparison: the cost of a social shock to a student is rarely injury. It is a broken schedule.

Within 48 hours the French prime minister had asked provincial prefectures to impose curfews where needed. A curfew closes the library at the hour you planned to use it. A transport shutdown cancels the exam you booked a month ago. A burned town hall is a building where residence permits and address registrations do not get processed that week. When Belgian students protested education funding, the disruption landed inside universities rather than outside them.

For a citizen, that is an inconvenience absorbed over a lifetime. For an international student on a twelve-month visa with a registration deadline written into an admission letter, a three-week processing gap can end a semester.

So the question worth asking about any destination is not "how many crimes happen there." It is: how likely is it that the ordinary machinery of daily life keeps running on the day you need it?

What the data can and cannot tell you

Two sources are worth looking at, with clear limits.

Numbeo's 2026 Safety Index — a crowd-sourced perception index, not police data — places China at 76.9, effectively tied with Japan at 77.2. For reference: Germany 61.6, Spain 62.4, Italy 52.7, the United Kingdom 51.7, the United States 50.8, Belgium 50.8, France 44.2. Because Numbeo asks respondents how safe they feel, it partly measures media coverage rather than conditions. Read it as a direction, not a measurement.

Gallup's 2025 Global Safety Report, drawn from about 145,000 adults across 144 countries and territories, found that 94% of respondents in China said they felt safe walking alone at night, against 71% in the United States. China's Law and Order Index score was 93, fourth worldwide; the UK and US both scored 84.

Then there is the one figure that is hard to argue with. Using UNODC data cited by the World Bank, intentional homicide in China runs below 1 per 100,000 — around 0.44 in 2024. That is not a perception survey. It is a count of bodies.

The caveats are real, and they cut both ways. Every country's crime statistics have recording problems, and China's official figures have been criticised for under-reporting, particularly by scholars who argue local police have incentives to keep numbers low. The reason the overall picture holds is not that the government says so: it is that perception surveys, tourism inflows and the homicide series are produced by different institutions with different interests, and they point the same way.

The four risks that are actually real in China

This is the section most agency content skips, which is exactly why it is worth reading. If violent crime is not your main risk in China, what is?

1. Traffic — mostly silent electric scooters

Two-wheeled electric vehicles are quiet, fast, frequently unlit at night, and they use pavements and bike lanes as a matter of course. Road traffic is the single most common cause of serious injury among international students in China. If you come from a right-hand-traffic country, look left and right at every crossing, because the first vehicle to reach you is usually a scooter coming from the direction you did not check.

2. Fraud aimed specifically at foreigners

The standard set repeats itself every semester: a video call from someone claiming to be a police officer who needs a transfer to "verify" your funds; a currency exchange offered at a rate better than any bank's; a landlord who wants a deposit before a viewing; a "part-time job" that consists of receiving money and forwarding it. Two rules block most of it. No government agency in China asks for payment by bank transfer, gift card or cryptocurrency, ever. And you never move money on behalf of a stranger, however plausible the story.

3. Medical payment, not medical quality

Chinese public hospitals are fast and competent, and the international clinic costs more than the local queue while being easier to navigate in English. What surprises students is the payment model: settlement is expected at the point of service, so without insurance you will be asked to pay before treatment rather than after. Buy the student insurance plan before you land, and check whether it covers outpatient visits and dental, which the cheapest plans do not.

4. Contract and visa literacy

Working part-time without the correct annotation on your residence permit is a violation, not a grey area you can talk your way out of. Subletting a room without the landlord registering it is the same. Most stories of students "in trouble with the authorities" turn out to be paperwork, not crime — and paperwork is the one risk on this list you can eliminate entirely by asking the international office before you sign anything.

Two of those six rows are fears that almost never materialise. Two are administrative. One is financial. Only one is physical — and it involves a scooter, not a stranger.

That distribution is the real argument. A country where violent crime is rare but paperwork is unforgiving is a different proposition from a country where the reverse is true, and the two demand completely different preparation. Choosing China means spending your attention on insurance, contract terms and traffic rather than on avoiding certain streets after dark.

Six things to do in your first week

Do them in this order. The first two have deadlines.

  1. Register your residence. Temporary accommodation must be registered with the local police station, usually within 24 hours of arrival. Universities normally handle it for dormitory students — confirm that they have, rather than assuming.
  2. Activate your insurance. Save the claim hotline and the hospital list in your phone before you need them, not after.
  3. Get a local number and set up mobile payment. WeChat Pay and Alipay are how taxis, canteens, hospitals and campus services are paid for. Without them, ordinary errands become significantly harder.
  4. Photograph your documents. Passport photo page, visa or residence permit, admission letter, insurance policy. Store them in cloud storage and keep one printed copy in your bag.
  5. Walk your route to campus once at night. Not for safety in the sense you are imagining — to find which crossings have no working lights and which ones feed scooters onto the pavement.
  6. Ask the international office three questions in writing. Can I work part-time, and how many hours? What exactly do I need on my residence permit to do it? Who signs a lease so that it is registered? Get answers by email so you have them later.

The bottom line

Europe's October weekend does not make European universities unsafe, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. What it does is expose a variable that rankings ignore: whether the systems around a student — transport, municipal offices, campus administration — keep working when the country around them is under stress.

On that specific measure China scores unusually well, and the reason is structural rather than cultural: dense policing, near-total CCTV coverage, a firearms ban, and administrative machinery that does not stop. Those same structures are why privacy is a live debate there, and that trade-off is something each student should weigh honestly rather than have decided for them.

The useful conclusion is narrower than "China is safe." It is this: in China, the things most likely to damage your year are a contract you did not read, an insurance policy you did not buy, and a road you did not look both ways on. All three are under your control. Very little of what actually goes wrong for an international student there requires luck to avoid — which is a more reassuring sentence than any safety index.
