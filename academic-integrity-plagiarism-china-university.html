Academic Integrity Rules at Chinese Universities: Plagiarism and Penalties

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-17

Chinese universities treat academic misconduct as a regulatory matter. Two ministerial orders define the offences and the procedures, a national similarity-checking system supplies the evidence, and penalties run from a recorded warning to the revocation of a degree years after graduation.

The Two Regulations That Define the Offence

Academic misconduct in China is governed by MOE Order No. 34, the Measures for Handling Fraud in Degree Theses, in force since 1 January 2013, and MOE Order No. 40, the Measures for Preventing and Handling Academic Misconduct in Higher Education Institutions, effective 1 September 2016. Order 34 covers degree theses; Order 40 covers research, papers and grants, and sets a six-month deadline for investigation.

What Order 34 actually says

Order 34 lists three punishable acts: buying, selling or commissioning a thesis; plagiarism or fabrication of data in a thesis; and falsifying the thesis process. A confirmed case leads to the degree being revoked, and the graduate is barred from reapplying for three years. If the offence is discovered after graduation, the degree is still cancelled and the employer is notified.

What Order 40 adds

Order 40 defines seven categories of misconduct, including plagiarism (剽窃), fabrication (伪造), falsification (篡改), improper authorship, duplicate submission, ghostwriting and other conduct that damages academic reputation. It requires a formal investigation, a defence hearing and a report to the provincial education department.

What Counts as Plagiarism Here

Plagiarism in the Chinese regulatory sense is broader than unattributed copying. It includes reusing your own previously published work without disclosure, submitting the same paper to two journals, listing a supervisor as co-author without contribution, and paraphrasing a Chinese-language source into English while citing only the English literature. The last pattern is the most common problem for international students.

Translation is not paraphrase

Translating a Chinese article into English and citing the original Chinese source is legitimate. Translating it and citing nothing, or citing a different English paper that says something similar, is plagiarism under Order 40. Detection is easier than students assume: library databases index both languages.

Ghostwriting and the contract-cheating market

Commissioned essays (代写) are explicitly listed as fraud under Order 34 when they form part of a degree thesis, and universities now run interview-based verification and draft-version checks. Students who paid for a thesis have been expelled after failing to explain their methodology in a viva.

The Similarity Check Everyone Talks About

The similarity report, or 查重, is produced by systems such as CNKI (知网), VIP (维普) and Wanfang, and the percentage is compared against a faculty threshold. Undergraduate theses are commonly capped at 15–20 percent, master's theses at 10–15 percent, and some doctoral programmes at 5–10 percent. Exceeding the cap triggers revision, a second check, or referral to the academic committee.

Why the number is not a target

The threshold counts matched strings, including references, quoted definitions and standard method descriptions. A student who writes original analysis but copies a methods section verbatim can fail a 15 percent cap while a student who cites heavily but paraphrases passes. The fix is to paraphrase method descriptions and quote sparingly with quotation marks.

Exams, Invigilation and the National Rules

Exam halls in China are invigilated strictly: phones must be surrendered or left outside, seating is fixed, and invigilators walk the rows. A phone discovered on your person during an exam is treated as possession of cheating material regardless of whether it was used, and the standard penalty is a demerit with a note in the student file.

National exams carry heavier consequences

For national examinations such as the HSK, the postgraduate entrance exam or CSC-funded programme tests, the national rules on handling exam violations apply. Cheating can produce a one to three year ban, recorded across sittings. For a scholarship holder, a national exam violation usually ends the scholarship independently of the university's own process.

Referencing: Where Foreign Students Slip

Chinese citation practice follows GB/T 7714, the national bibliographic standard, which numbers references in citation order and formats them differently from APA or Harvard. Faculties usually accept either GB/T 7714 or the style used in your discipline, but they require consistency, and a reference list mixing both formats reads as careless rather than international.

Practical rules that avoid trouble

Keep a copy of every source you read, with the page numbers, at the moment you read it. Chinese databases do not always provide stable page numbers for scanned editions, so note the chapter instead. If you translate a passage, mark it in your notes as a translation and cite the original, because that note is your defence if the similarity report later flags the passage.

AI Tools After the 2023 Rules

China's interim measures on generative AI services took effect on 15 August 2023 and require AI-generated content to be labelled, and most universities have issued their own student guidance since 2024. The common position permits AI for language polishing and brainstorming but prohibits submitting generated text as your own analysis, and several faculties now require a declaration of AI use with the thesis.

The declaration is the real control

Where a declaration is required, an undeclared AI-assisted chapter is treated as a false statement about authorship rather than as a tooling issue. Declaring polishing help costs nothing; discovering it later costs a disciplinary record. Ask your supervisor in week one which declaration your faculty uses; the form differs between schools.

How to Stay Clean

The habits that prevent a case are boring and take about an hour a week, and they all work by producing evidence before anyone asks for it. Source records, draft versions and a declared AI position answer the three questions an academic committee actually investigates: where the text came from, whether the student wrote it, and whether the process was disclosed. In practice that means:

  1. Record every source with page or chapter numbers as you read it.
  2. Quote directly only with quotation marks and a page reference.
  3. Paraphrase method sections rather than copying standard procedures.
  4. Run the free pre-check most libraries offer before the official 查重.
  5. Keep draft versions, since they are the evidence that the work is yours.
  6. Declare AI assistance if your faculty requires it, before submission.
  7. Ask your supervisor about citation style in the first meeting, not the last week.

The regulatory machinery exists because degree fraud damaged the credibility of Chinese qualifications in the 2000s, and it is enforced more consistently now than most incoming students expect.
