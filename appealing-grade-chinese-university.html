Appealing a Grade at a Chinese University

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-26

A disappointing mark at a Chinese university feels like something to argue about, but the system is built to be checked rather than debated. There is a formal review route, it is genuinely open to international students, and it produces real corrections — just not the ones most students are hoping for. Understanding what the process can and cannot change is the difference between a fixed transcript and a lost semester.

A grade review checks arithmetic, not judgement

Grade review (成绩复核) verifies that every answer was marked, that the marks were added correctly, that the total was entered into the system correctly, and that the right student's name is attached. It does not re-mark your essay, does not reconsider whether an answer deserved three points instead of two, and does not weigh your attendance. Those are academic judgements, and the system protects them.

The four things a review can find

Missed pages at the back of a booklet, an unmarked section, a transcription error between the marking sheet and the system, and a name or student-number mix-up. Each of those is objective and verifiable. If your complaint is that the marking was harsh, you are asking for the one thing the process explicitly excludes, and the request will be closed with a one-line reply.

Why the scope is deliberately narrow

Chinese universities treat the examiner's academic judgement as final because the alternative is re-marking every disputed paper by committee, which no institution can sustain. The narrow scope is also a protection: it means a review cannot lower your mark, only confirm or correct it, so there is no downside to asking.

The window is days, not weeks

Most universities accept a grade review request within five working days of the result being published, and some allow only three. The deadline is counted from the publication date in the system, not from the day you noticed, and it does not pause for weekends or for your being off campus. Miss it and the mark becomes final regardless of merit.

Where the deadline is published

The course handbook, the school's notice board and the academic affairs office website each carry it, and they do not always agree. Ask your class teacher in writing, because a screenshot of a message stating the deadline is the only evidence that will reopen a closed case. Do this in the first week of the semester, before any result is in dispute.

Filing it correctly

The request is a form from the school office, not an email to the lecturer, though a polite email to the lecturer is worth sending in parallel. Attach the paper or the assignment if you have it, state which question and which mark you are disputing, and say what the correct total should be. Vague requests are returned; specific ones get a calculator applied to them.

The escalation path has four gates

The route runs from the examiner outward, and each gate has a different authority. Skipping a gate does not speed things up, because the body above it will send the file back down. The four stages below come from the university's own procedure and from Ministry of Education Order No. 41, which governs student appeals nationally.

  1. Ask the course lecturer, in writing, for the marking breakdown of the specific question.
  2. Submit the formal review request to the school office within the published window.
  3. If the school confirms the mark, appeal to the academic affairs office with the paper trail.
  4. If that fails, file with the student appeals committee within 10 days of the decision.
  5. As a final step, appeal to the provincial education department within 15 days of the committee's finding.

What a successful review actually changes

The outcome is narrower than students expect and more useful than they fear. A confirmed error changes the recorded mark, and the corrected mark flows into the transcript, the GPA calculation and the scholarship review automatically once the office posts it. Nothing else in your record moves.

Where international students lose these cases

Three failures account for most unsuccessful appeals. The first is timing: by the time a student understands the mark was wrong, the five-day window has closed. The second is framing: a request written as a complaint about fairness rather than a request to check a calculation is closed on arrival. The third is evidence: without the marked paper, the office has nothing to check.

The language problem

Reviews are conducted in Chinese, on documents written in Chinese, and the reply will be in Chinese. Ask the international students office for help drafting the request, and keep a copy of the Chinese version you submitted. A translated summary of your own argument is not the same document, and it will not be treated as one.

The attendance trap

Many Chinese courses require 70 per cent attendance to sit the final examination, and an absence record can produce a fail that looks like a marking error. Check the attendance log before you file, because if the fail is procedural the review will confirm it, and your real remedy is the absence appeal rather than the grade appeal.

When to accept the mark and retake instead

If the review window has closed, or the problem is genuinely one of judgement, the practical route is the make-up examination or the retake. A failed course normally allows one make-up sitting (补考), usually recorded at 60 marks regardless of the actual score, and a second failure means repeating the course. Both routes cost time, and the retake route costs tuition per credit at most universities.

Knowing which fight you are in is the whole skill. A wrong total is a clerical problem with a clean fix; a 58 that you believe should be 62 is a judgement call that no committee in China will overturn, and spending a month on it delays the retake that would actually repair your transcript.
