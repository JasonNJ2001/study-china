"Halal, Vegetarian and Campus Dining in China"

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-10

Food is the first daily problem an international student solves in China, and it is solved on campus, in a subsidised canteen, for less than the price of a coffee in Europe. The complication is not availability but labelling: halal is clearly marked, vegetarian almost never is, and the difference matters more than most guidebooks admit.

Is halal food available at Chinese universities?

Yes. Most universities with a Muslim student body run a separate halal canteen (清真食堂) with its own kitchen, utensils and staff, and it is usually the cheapest reliable option on campus. Lanzhou, Ningxia, Xinjiang, Xi'an and Yunnan universities all operate dedicated halal halls, and smaller campuses run at least a halal counter.

The halal canteen is not a concession to foreigners; it exists because China has roughly 20 million Muslim citizens, and universities in every province serve Hui, Uyghur, Kazak and Salar students. That is good news for a Malaysian, Indonesian, Pakistani or Nigerian student, because the infrastructure predates you by decades.

How to check before you enrol

Look for 清真食堂 in the campus dining list, or ask the international students office to confirm the number of halal outlets and whether they open during vacations. A campus with a halal hall but no vacation service is a real problem in February, when Chinese New Year closes everything else.

What the halal mark guarantees

A certified halal canteen buys from suppliers with a halal certificate, keeps separate woks and chopping boards, and does not serve pork or alcohol. Street stalls displaying 清真 usually follow the same rule by reputation rather than by audit, and a stall with the sign but no separate kitchen is a judgement call you have to make yourself.

Eating vegetarian in a Chinese canteen

Vegetarian food exists everywhere but is rarely labelled, and the obstacles are condiments rather than meat. Lard, oyster sauce, dried shrimp, chicken essence and bone stock appear in dishes that look plant-based, so a plate of stir-fried greens may contain four animal derivatives before you notice any.

The practical solution is a script you can repeat. Learn 我吃素 (wo chi su, I eat vegetarian) and then the three refusals: 不要肉 (no meat), 不要猪油 (no lard), 不要蚝油 (no oyster sauce). Say it before ordering, not after, and point at the dish you want.

The Buddhist restaurant shortcut

Every city with a temple has cheap vegetarian restaurants attached to it, and Chinese Buddhist cuisine (斋菜) is fully plant-based by doctrine, with mock meats made from wheat gluten, konjac and tofu. They are more expensive than a canteen and far more reliable than a random restaurant, which makes them useful when you are travelling.

What a campus meal actually costs

A canteen meal costs RMB 8-20, and three canteen meals a day for a month lands between RMB 800 and RMB 1,500. That is the single biggest reason campus food matters to your budget, because off-campus restaurants charge two to three times more for the same plate.

Canteens are subsidised by the university and by the municipal government, and the subsidy is invisible: it appears as a bowl of noodles for RMB 9 that would cost RMB 25 outside the gate. Cooking in a dorm is usually banned for fire safety, so the canteen is not one option among many, it is the option.

The campus card problem

Almost no canteen accepts cash. You load a campus card (一卡通) at the card centre or through WeChat, and the same card opens your dorm, borrows library books and pays for laundry, so activate it in your first week rather than your first hungry evening.

Top-ups are usually instant in the app and slower at the machine. Keep a cash balance of RMB 100-200 on the card rather than RMB 20, because the queue at the top-up desk at 12:05 is longer than the queue for food.

Ramadan, fasting and exam weeks

During Ramadan, halal canteens at many universities extend opening hours to before dawn and after sunset, and the international office can confirm the schedule. If fasting overlaps with exam week, tell your teacher early, because Chinese universities grant deferred exams for documented religious observance more readily when asked in advance.

Dormitory kitchens are mostly absent, so the pre-dawn meal depends on the halal canteen or on food you keep in your room. A small rice cooker is technically banned in most dormitories but widely owned; an electric kettle and shelf-stable food are the compliant version of the same solution.

Delivery apps and off-campus options

Meituan and Ele.me dominate delivery, with campus drop-off points and average delivery in 30-40 minutes, but their vegetarian filters are unreliable. Search by dish name instead, and pair 素食 with a specific vegetable, because a generic vegetarian search returns meat dishes that happen to contain vegetables.

Delivery fees are small, RMB 2-6, and the apps run frequent discounts that make a delivered meal competitive with a canteen one. What they do not solve is certification: a restaurant that says vegetarian online and cooks with lard in the kitchen is a risk you cannot see from the app.

Your first month, in order

  1. Find the halal canteen or the vegetarian counter on day one, before you need it.
  2. Activate the campus card and load RMB 200, not RMB 20.
  3. Learn the four phrases and use them at the same counter until staff recognise you.
  4. Ask the international office which canteens stay open during winter vacation and Spring Festival.
  5. Bookmark two off-campus vegetarian or halal restaurants within walking distance, for the days the canteen fails.
  6. Cook nothing in the dorm that trips the fire alarm; the fine is real and the paperwork is worse.

Eating in China is easier than the internet suggests and harder than a campus brochure claims. The infrastructure is there, the labelling is not, and the students who eat well are the ones who learn eight characters of Chinese and one sentence about lard.
