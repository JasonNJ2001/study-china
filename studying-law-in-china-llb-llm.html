Studying Law in China: LLB and LLM Programmes for International Students

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-20

Law is one of the few degrees in China where the language requirement is not a formality. Legal Chinese is a different register from conversational Mandarin, and a contract clause does not forgive a misread character. That single fact shapes every programme choice below, from the four-year LLB taught entirely in Chinese to the English-taught LLMs designed for foreign lawyers who will never practise Chinese law.

What a Chinese law degree actually is

A Chinese law degree is a professional undergraduate qualification, not a graduate one: students enter a 法学学士 (LLB) at eighteen and study for four years, and the LLM exists mainly for people who already hold a law degree. Foreign applicants therefore choose between joining the undergraduate track in Chinese or taking a master's programme built around comparative and international law.

That inversion surprises applicants from common-law countries, where law is postgraduate. In China the LLB is the mainstream route into the profession, the LLM is a specialisation, and a foreign student holding a non-law bachelor's degree can still be admitted to an LLM provided the programme accepts conversion students, which roughly half do.

Four programme types, four different outcomes

The four routes differ in length, language of instruction and what the graduate can do afterwards, and the differences are larger than the tuition gap suggests. A four-year LLB in Chinese produces a lawyer who can read Chinese statutes; a one-year English-taught LLM produces a lawyer who can explain Chinese law to foreign clients but cannot draft in it.

The four-year LLB

The LLB covers constitutional law, civil law, criminal law, civil procedure, administrative law and jurisprudence, usually with a compulsory internship and a thesis in year four. Entry requires HSK 5 at most universities and HSK 6 at the strongest ones, plus an interview that tests whether you can follow a lecture on 物权法 without switching to English.

The Chinese-taught LLM

A Chinese-taught LLM runs two to three years and is the route for students who intend to sit a Chinese postgraduate entrance exam or work inside a Chinese firm. Coursework is seminar-based and assessed by long written papers, and the thesis is expected to engage with Chinese-language scholarship rather than translate it.

The English-taught LLM

English-taught LLMs at Peking University, Tsinghua, Shanghai Jiao Tong's Koguan School of Law and East China University of Political Science and Law attract working lawyers and compliance officers. They cover Chinese business law, international arbitration and WTO rules, and they assume no Mandarin, which makes them the only realistic option for a student who wants to start within twelve months.

The J.D. route at Peking University's Shenzhen campus

Peking University School of Transnational Law, in Shenzhen, teaches a US-style curriculum in English with American casebooks and a common-law method, and is the only programme of its kind inside the Chinese system. It is expensive, selective and designed for students who want to sit a US bar examination rather than a Chinese one.

Language requirements decide everything

Law programmes quote HSK 5 as the floor, which means 180 points on a 300-point scale, but the floor is not the working level. Reading a Chinese judgment requires recognition of classical constructions and administrative vocabulary that HSK 5 does not test, and most programmes quietly expect HSK 6 for anything involving Chinese-language coursework.

Vocabulary is the real bottleneck rather than grammar. A student who passes HSK 6 can still stall in a criminal procedure seminar because 取保候审, 附带民事诉讼 and 二审终审 are not everyday words. Ask the admissions office whether the programme teaches legal Chinese in the first semester; the good ones do, as a separate compulsory module.

What the degree does not give you

Foreign nationals cannot sit the 国家统一法律职业资格考试, the unified legal profession qualification examination, because eligibility is tied to Chinese nationality. A foreign graduate therefore cannot qualify as a Chinese lawyer, cannot appear in a Chinese court, and cannot sign a Chinese legal opinion, regardless of how well they performed in the LLB.

What the degree does give you is deep access: the ability to read primary sources, work alongside Chinese counsel, and explain a Chinese regulatory position to a client abroad without a translator. Plan the career around that advantage, not around a certificate you will not obtain.

Cost, scholarships and the CSC route

Tuition sits between CNY 26,000 and 45,000 a year for Chinese-taught programmes and roughly double that for English-taught LLMs, with living costs of CNY 1,500–2,500 a month in Beijing or Shanghai. The Chinese Government Scholarship covers tuition, dormitory and a monthly stipend, and law sits inside the eligible subject list at most host universities.

The CSC window opens in January and usually closes between late March and mid-April, and applicants apply either through their national ministry of education or directly to a designated university under the Type B channel. Provincial scholarships, notably in Jiangsu, Zhejiang and Guangdong, run later and are less competitive, and most law schools hold a small number of partial tuition waivers for returning applicants.

Careers where the degree actually pays

The graduates who do well are not the ones with the best grades but the ones who combine Chinese legal training with a home jurisdiction. Chinese firms hiring foreign counsel want someone who can review a joint venture contract in both languages, and that skill commands a premium in arbitration, trade compliance and intellectual property.

Typical destinations are the foreign desks of Chinese firms in Beijing and Shanghai, the China practice of international firms, and in-house compliance at manufacturers with export exposure. Salaries start below consulting but rise faster once you are the person who can read the other side's statute.

How to apply without losing a year

Applications for September entry close between December and April depending on the university, and the LLM deadlines sit later than the LLB ones. The sequence below reflects what actually causes delays: documents first, then the language certificate, then the visa, and only then the travel booking.

  1. Confirm whether the programme teaches in Chinese or English before anything else.
  2. Book the HSK test date, since results take about four weeks to reach the admissions office.
  3. Collect a sealed transcript, degree certificate and two academic references.
  4. Write a personal statement that names a specific area of Chinese law, not "law in general".
  5. Submit through the university portal rather than an agent, and keep the application number.
  6. Apply for the X1 visa as soon as the JW202 form arrives, not after the flight is booked.

A well-prepared applicant can move from decision to enrolment in five months. The ones who lose a year are almost always waiting on a language certificate they could have booked in October.
