Spring Intake at Chinese Universities: January and March Start Dates

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-20

Almost every guide to studying in China assumes a September start. In practice a second intake exists, smaller and less advertised, running from late February to mid-March. It is the difference between waiting eleven months and starting in four, and it comes with its own visa logic and funding limits.

Which Chinese universities actually run a spring intake

Spring admission is widespread for non-degree Chinese language programmes and patchy for degrees. Most comprehensive universities admit language students in both March and September, a smaller group admits master's students in spring, and very few admit undergraduates in March. Check the international students office page rather than a third-party portal, because agents list spring options that closed months ago.

Universities that consistently run a March intake for language study include Beijing Language and Culture University, Fudan, Zhejiang and most provincial normal universities. Degree-level spring admission is concentrated in business, engineering and Chinese-taught master's programmes, and it is usually described as 春季入学.

Why the spring cycle is invisible on ranking sites

Ranking and comparison sites index degree programmes, not intakes, so a university can run a busy March language cohort without it appearing anywhere except its own Chinese-language page. Check the 国际学生 page and the 招生简章 PDF before assuming a March start is available.

September versus March: the two calendars compared

The two intakes differ in application timing, programme coverage and funding, and applicants routinely underestimate how early the spring cycle begins. A March start means applying between September and December of the previous year, which is roughly when most people are still deciding whether to go at all.

The calendar consequence is worth spelling out: a student who misses the April deadline for September does not wait another year. They can enter in March, complete a semester of Chinese, and roll into the September intake ahead of classmates who arrived cold.

The four applicants who benefit most

Spring intake rewards specific situations rather than being a general second chance. If you recognise yourself in the list below, the March route is probably worth the extra paperwork; if you do not, waiting for September usually produces a better outcome and a stronger application.

  1. Students who missed the September deadline by weeks rather than months and do not want a full gap year.
  2. Applicants who need a semester of Chinese before a Chinese-taught degree begins.
  3. Exchange and study-abroad students whose home university runs a spring semester abroad.
  4. Transfer students whose current programme ends in December.

A fifth group exists but should be treated cautiously: students who want to test China before committing to a degree. A spring semester does that cheaply, but credits rarely transfer without an agreement.

Language programmes are the real spring market

Non-degree Chinese language programmes are the core of the March intake and the least standardised. Length ranges from eight weeks to a full academic year, class sizes from eight to twenty-five, and weekly hours from twelve to twenty-four. A one-semester programme usually runs about eighteen weeks and finishes in early July.

Prices cluster between CNY 8,000 and 16,000 per semester at public universities, with Beijing and Shanghai at the top. Ask three questions before paying: how many hours per week, whether classes are streamed by HSK level, and whether the programme issues the JW202 form needed for an X1 visa rather than only an X2.

Length, hours and class streaming

A cheap programme with twelve hours a week and thirty students per class is not the same product as one with twenty hours and streamed levels. HSK 3 to HSK 5 in a single semester is realistic at twenty hours and optimistic at twelve.

Visas, JW202 and the 180-day rule

The visa type follows the length of the programme, not the intake. A course longer than 180 days takes an X1 visa, which is converted into a residence permit within thirty days of arrival; a course of 180 days or less takes an X2, which cannot be converted and cannot be extended inside China. A spring semester of eighteen weeks sits just under the line, which is why so many one-semester students arrive on X2.

The 180 days are counted from the official start and end dates on the admission letter, so a semester running from late February to early July stays under the threshold while one with a two-week orientation added on top may not. If you plan to continue into a degree in September, ask for a programme length that crosses 180 days so you can obtain an X1 and convert it.

When an X2 is the right choice

A single semester, a short exchange or a summer school of under six months all fit X2, which is cheaper and faster to obtain than X1. The trade-off is that X2 cannot be converted or extended inside China, so you must leave and re-apply if you decide to stay on.

Funding: what spring intake does not offer

The Chinese Government Scholarship operates a single main intake in September, with applications opening in January and closing between late March and mid-April. Confucius Institute Scholarships follow the same September pattern. A student entering in March is therefore almost always self-funded for the first semester.

Partial exceptions exist. Provincial scholarships in Jiangsu, Zhejiang and Sichuan occasionally fund spring language study, and university-level tuition waivers are sometimes available for students who convert from a language programme into a degree. Ask the international office directly; these awards are rarely advertised on the website and are usually allocated late, when September numbers fall short.

Where partial spring funding still exists

The awards worth chasing are small and local: a 50% tuition waiver at a provincial normal university, or a dormitory fee exemption for the first semester. The international office decides them, so one email to the right person beats a formal application.

How to apply for March without losing a year

The spring cycle moves faster than the autumn one and tolerates fewer mistakes, because there is no second round to fall back on. The sequence below is ordered by what actually blocks an application, starting with the document that takes longest to obtain.

  1. Shortlist three universities that confirm a March start on their own website, not on an agent's page.
  2. Request a sealed transcript and a bank statement covering the last six months.
  3. Book the HSK test if the programme requires a level; results take about four weeks.
  4. Submit the application between September and November, and keep the reference number.
  5. Pay the application fee by the method the portal specifies, and keep the receipt.
  6. Apply for the visa as soon as the JW202 arrives, allowing three weeks for processing.

Timing is the whole game: September applications are decided by November, leaving room for a visa; December applications often return an admission letter in February.
