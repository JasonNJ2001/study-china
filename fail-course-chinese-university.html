"What Happens If You Fail a Course at a Chinese University"

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-10

Failing a course in China is not a quiet academic event. It is recorded by a registry, it triggers a sequence of administrative steps with fixed dates, and it can reach your scholarship, your residence permit and your graduation certificate before you have finished reading the email that announced it.

What counts as failing a course in China?

A course is failed below 60 out of 100, or below a 1.0 grade point on a 4.0 scale, and the result is recorded by the registry rather than negotiated with the teacher. Attendance below two thirds of scheduled hours also produces an automatic fail, even when the coursework was excellent.

There is no curve and no discretion at most universities. The teacher submits a score, the department approves it, the registry publishes it, and the number that appears is the number that stays. If a score looks wrong, the route is a formal review, not a conversation.

The make-up exam and the 60 ceiling

One make-up exam (补考) is normally offered at the start of the next semester, and the score is capped at 60 regardless of what you actually earn. That cap is the hidden cost: the course is passed, but the 60 stays on the transcript and drags the GPA you will send to employers and graduate schools.

Make-up exams are usually scheduled in the week before teaching resumes, with about two weeks of notice. Missing one without a documented medical reason converts the fail into a mandatory retake, and the fee that goes with it.

Retaking a course, and what it costs

A second failure means a retake (重修), where you attend the course again from the beginning and pay a fee per credit, commonly RMB 100-300. Retakes run alongside your normal load, so a failed 4-credit course adds four weekly periods and up to RMB 1,200 to an already full semester.

Some universities let you sit the retake exam without attending the lectures if the timetable clashes, and this is worth asking about in writing. Others require full attendance again, which effectively adds a course to your semester and can push you over the credit ceiling.

The dismissal threshold varies by university and is usually expressed as a proportion of total credits, for example one third of the credits attempted in a year. It is not a rumour: it appears in the student handbook, and it has been applied.

What it does to your scholarship

A failed course can cost more than tuition. CSC funding is renewed through an annual review (年度评审) that weighs academic performance, and a failure can suspend or cancel the stipend even though your enrolment continues. Provincial scholarships usually renew on the same logic with a lower tolerance.

The asymmetry catches people out. Losing the stipend does not cancel your visa, so you keep studying and start paying tuition and rent from your own funds, mid-degree, on a student budget built around money that has stopped arriving.

What the annual review actually scores

The review form is short and the categories are broad: academic record, learning attitude, conduct and participation in university activities. A single failed course is not an automatic cancellation, but it removes the margin, and a second one in the same year usually ends the funding rather than suspending it.

Can you appeal a grade?

Grade appeals exist but they are narrow. You can request a review (成绩复核) within a set window, usually five to ten working days after results are published, and the review checks whether marks were added correctly rather than whether the teacher was fair.

Procedural errors succeed: a missing page, a misrecorded score, a component weighted wrongly. Disagreement with the marking standard does not, and a review that turns up nothing leaves the original score standing. Ask for the raw mark breakdown before you file, because the answer often explains the number.

What is worth contesting

  • A final exam page that was not marked, visible in the script.
  • Coursework marks missing from the totals, with submission receipts.
  • A weighting that contradicts the syllabus distributed in week one.
  • An absence recorded while you were on approved university business.
  • A score entered for the wrong student, which happens more than universities admit.

Consequences beyond the transcript

Failed credits delay graduation, and a delayed graduation invalidates the end date on your residence permit, which must then be extended while you are no longer a full-time student in the normal sense. That extension is possible but requires the school's cooperation and a written explanation.

The residence permit timing problem

A residence permit is issued against an expected graduation date, and that date moves when credits are incomplete. The extension is granted, but it requires a letter from the school and a visit to the exit and entry administration before the old permit expires, not after.

Failed credits also break the credit total that a master's application expects to see. Chinese transcripts list every attempt, so a retaken course often shows both scores, and an admissions committee abroad will read the first one. It is explainable, but it has to be explained.

How to recover without losing a year

  1. Read the assessment rules in week one and note the two-thirds attendance line.
  2. Register for the make-up exam as soon as the date is published, not when a friend mentions it.
  3. Ask the teacher for the mark breakdown in writing before requesting a review.
  4. If you must retake, check whether lecture attendance is required or only the exam.
  5. Tell the international office in the same week, before the registry contacts them.
  6. Recalculate your credit load and drop an elective rather than carrying five courses and failing two.

Most students who fail one course still graduate on time. The ones who lose a year are usually those who ignore the make-up date, hide the failure from the international office, or add a retake on top of a full load and fail something else in the same semester.
