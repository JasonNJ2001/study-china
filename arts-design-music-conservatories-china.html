Arts, Design and Music Conservatories in China for International Students

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-19

China's art education is split between two kinds of institution that behave very differently for foreign applicants: the independent academies, which run their own entrance exams and studio culture, and the art or music schools inside comprehensive universities, which are easier to enter but carry less industry weight. Choosing between them is the first real decision, and it shapes everything after.

The two routes into art education in China

The independent academies, including CAFA in Beijing and CAA in Hangzhou, admit through their own portfolio review and studio tests, and they carry the strongest reputation with galleries, studios and design employers. Art schools inside comprehensive universities admit through a lighter process, often with no studio exam for international applicants, and suit students who want a broader academic campus.

The difference shows up in hiring. Chinese design studios and galleries recruit heavily from the eight independent art academies and the major conservatories, because those schools' studio culture and alumni networks are concentrated. A comprehensive-university art degree is a legitimate qualification, but it does not open the same doors in Shanghai or Shenzhen.

The eight independent art academies

The group usually called the eight academies comprises CAFA and Tsinghua Academy of Arts and Design in Beijing, CAA in Hangzhou, Lu Xun Academy in Shenyang, Sichuan Fine Arts Institute in Chongqing, Guangzhou Academy, Xi'an Academy and Tianjin Academy. Each has a distinct specialism: CAFA for painting and curation, CAA for ink and interdisciplinary media, Tsinghua for design and textiles, Guangzhou for commercial design and animation.

The conservatories

Music follows the same pattern. Central Conservatory of Music and China Conservatory in Beijing, Shanghai Conservatory, Xinghai in Guangzhou, Sichuan in Chengdu, Wuhan, Shenyang, Tianjin and Xi'an each run independent auditions. Composition, performance and Chinese traditional instruments are the strongest areas, and the application requires a recorded audition plus, for shortlisted candidates, a live or remote practical test.

Which schools take international students, and how

Most of the eight academies and all the major conservatories enrol international students through a separate channel from Chinese nationals, which means you usually skip the provincial art exam and sit the school's own assessment instead. That channel is smaller and less advertised, so you have to ask the international office directly rather than rely on the Chinese-language admissions page.

What the portfolio and studio test actually involve

A typical international portfolio is 15 to 20 works with a written statement of 500 to 800 characters, and the studio test, where required, runs three to four hours on drawing from observation rather than imagination. Schools are looking for drawing discipline and a coherent line of enquiry, not polished renderings, and portfolios assembled by a paid studio are usually recognisable.

Submit images at 300 dpi with dimensions and medium listed, and include three or four process shots per project. Chinese assessors read process as evidence of thinking, which is why a sketchbook photographed page by page often carries more weight than a single finished piece.

Music auditions and theory requirements

Conservatory applicants submit a video of two or three contrasting pieces, then sit a theory and ear-training test that trips up many foreign candidates who prepared only repertoire. Chinese conservatories teach Western theory with Chinese solfège terminology, so brush up on intervals, modes and dictation before the test rather than after.

Language: HSK 4 is the floor, not the target

Chinese-taught art programmes generally require HSK 4 at 180 or above, and design schools inside top universities often ask for HSK 5. Studio criticism is delivered verbally and fast, so a student at the HSK 4 floor will understand instructions but miss the reasoning, which is where most of the learning sits. A small number of English-taught master's tracks exist, mostly in design and art history.

Budget a language year if you are starting from zero, and expect to pay CNY 12,000–18,000 for it at an academy's own language centre. Doing that year at the same school gives you studio access and a relationship with the tutors who will later assess your portfolio.

Costs, scholarships and the application calendar

International tuition at the independent academies runs about CNY 26,000–40,000 a year, against roughly CNY 15,000–20,000 for Chinese nationals, with materials and studio fees adding CNY 5,000–10,000 annually for painting and sculpture. Conservatory tuition sits in a similar range, and instrument maintenance or hire is a separate line.

Applications for the September intake usually open in December and close between March and April, with portfolio review in April and results by June. The Chinese Government Scholarship runs its own window from January to April, and a handful of academies have dedicated art scholarships routed through provincial governments.

Living where the industry is

City choice matters more in art than in most fields. Beijing concentrates galleries, museums and the state academy system; Shanghai and Hangzhou dominate design, fashion and new media; Guangzhou and Shenzhen offer animation, gaming and manufacturing-adjacent design work. Studios recruit locally, so graduating in the city where you want to work is a practical advantage rather than a lifestyle preference.

Rent and materials in Beijing and Shanghai run 30 to 50 per cent higher than in Shenyang or Xi'an, which is worth weighing against the weaker local industry in cheaper cities.

A realistic preparation checklist

Work backwards ten months from the application deadline, because portfolios take longer than applicants expect and HSK seats fill early. The six steps below assume you are targeting the September intake and have not yet booked a test date. Each step produces something a school can assess, rather than an intention to apply later:

  1. Decide between independent academy and comprehensive university based on the work you want to make, not on ranking tables.
  2. Photograph existing work properly and identify the four weakest pieces to replace.
  3. Start HSK preparation if you are below level 4, and book the test date early because seats fill.
  4. Email the international office for the foreign-applicant portfolio brief, which differs from the domestic one.
  5. Prepare a 500-character statement in Chinese describing your practice and why this school.
  6. For music, record two contrasting pieces in one continuous take and prepare theory dictation.

Art study in China rewards students who arrive with a practice rather than a portfolio, because the studio system assumes you will keep producing work under criticism. Pick the city and the school that match the work you actually want to make, and the paperwork becomes manageable.
