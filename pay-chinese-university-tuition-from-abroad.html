How to Pay Chinese University Tuition from Abroad

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-03

Tuition payment is the step where otherwise well-organised applicants lose money for no reason. A university quotes a number in yuan, a bank converts it at a rate with a margin, an intermediary takes a percentage, and the money arrives short of the required amount three weeks later.

The fix is knowing which routes the university actually accepts, and what proof of payment it will accept when the money lands.

The four routes universities accept

Universities accept four routes: an international SWIFT wire at 150 to 400 yuan plus correspondent charges, the university's own online portal at 0.5 to 2 per cent, a third-party platform such as Flywire or Convera at 1 to 3 per cent, and payment in person on arrival. Use only the route printed in your admission letter.

Wire transfer: cheapest for large sums, and the most error-prone

A SWIFT transfer to the university's corporate account is usually the cheapest route for a full year of tuition, because the fee is fixed rather than proportional. It is also where most problems occur.

Three details determine whether the money arrives intact. The beneficiary name must match the account name exactly, including the university's legal Chinese name in English transliteration. The SWIFT code must be the one on the invoice, not one found on a search engine. And the payment reference must contain your application number or student ID, because a Chinese university finance office receives hundreds of transfers in August and identifies them by reference, not by sender.

Instruct your bank to send the full invoiced amount with charges paid by the sender. If correspondent bank fees are deducted from the principal, the university receives less than the required figure and treats the payment as incomplete, which can delay your JW202 form.

Third-party platforms: convenient, and worth the fee

A platform such as Flywire or Convera quotes the exact amount that will reach the university in yuan before you confirm, which removes both the correspondent-bank guesswork and the reference-field mistakes. For applicants in countries where a bank wire to China needs three intermediary banks, the two to three per cent is usually money well spent. Confirm first that your university lists the platform, because a payment through an unlisted channel can take weeks to trace.

The receipt is not the transfer confirmation

This is the part students underestimate. The document that matters is the university's official receipt, issued after the finance office reconciles the incoming payment. A bank transfer confirmation proves you sent money. It does not prove the university received it, and it is the receipt that appears in your visa paperwork.

Ask the admissions office, in writing, how long reconciliation takes and what the receipt will look like. Ten working days is normal in the peak August window. If you are paying close to a deadline, pay earlier rather than paying faster.

What the receipt must show

  • Your full name as it appears in your passport
  • Your student ID or application number
  • The amount in yuan and the purpose, tuition or deposit
  • The university's official seal and the finance office's issue date

A screenshot from a payment platform, however official it looks, is not a substitute. Several universities will not issue the JW202 form until the sealed receipt exists.

Currency, timing and the exchange rate

Pay in yuan wherever the option exists, because a university quoting in dollars applies its own conversion at a rate worse than your bank's. Timing matters as much: invoices are issued in June and July for a September start, and a transfer initiated in late August can miss enrolment, one of the few grounds for withdrawing an offer.

Watch the calendar. Chinese universities issue invoices in June and July for a September start, and the finance offices process a year's worth of international payments in a six-week window. A transfer initiated in late August can miss the enrolment deadline entirely, and late payment is one of the few grounds on which an offer is withdrawn.

Deposits, refunds and the fine print

Most programmes ask for a deposit of 2,000 to 8,000 yuan to hold a place, credited against tuition later. Refund terms vary enormously: some return it minus a processing fee if you withdraw before a stated date, others treat it as non-refundable from the day it is paid, including when a visa is refused.

Ask the admissions office one direct question: if my visa is refused, is the deposit returned? A written answer to that single question is worth more than any brochure, and the refusal case is the one most often left out of the published clause.

Avoiding the payment scams that target applicants

The three red flags

  • An account holder name that is a person's name rather than the university or its finance office.
  • A request to pay through a messaging app to an individual, including someone claiming to be your admissions officer.
  • A discount for paying early through an unlisted channel, which no Chinese public university offers.

Confirm bank details with the admissions office through the contact address on the university's own domain, not through a reply in an email thread that arrived unprompted. Payment-redirect fraud targets international applicants precisely because they cannot walk into the office to check.

A payment checklist that prevents most problems

  1. Read the payment instructions in the admission letter and use only the listed route.
  2. Confirm the beneficiary account name, SWIFT code and reference format in writing.
  3. Send the full amount with sender-paid charges, and keep the bank's confirmation.
  4. Photograph or scan the sealed university receipt when it arrives, and keep two copies.
  5. Store the receipt with your admission letter and JW202 form, since all three are requested together at the visa stage.

Paying is the least interesting part of preparing to study in China, and the one most likely to cost you a semester. It rewards ten minutes of checking before the transfer rather than three weeks of chasing afterwards.
