Campus Recruitment and Job Fairs at Chinese Universities

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-19

Chinese universities run one of the most structured graduate recruitment systems in the world, and international students sit mostly outside it by default. The annual autumn campaign moves hundreds of thousands of graduates into jobs within eight weeks, but the fairs, portals and eligibility rules are built around Chinese nationals, so a foreign student needs to know which doors are actually open and when.

How campus recruitment works in China

Campus recruitment, called 校招, runs in two waves: autumn recruitment from September to November for students graduating the following June, and spring recruitment from March to April for anyone still unplaced. Employers hire through the school's employment centre, so your university's career office, not a job board, controls much of the access.

The autumn wave is the main event, because the largest state-owned enterprises, technology companies and banks fill most of their graduate quotas before December. Spring is smaller and is treated as a second chance, with more contract roles and fewer structured training programmes.

Autumn versus spring, in practice

Autumn recruitment begins in the first weeks of the academic year, often before final-year students have finished their coursework. Companies hold information sessions on campus in September, open online applications in September and October, run written tests and interviews through October and November, and issue offers before the year ends. Spring recruitment reopens in March for the roles autumn did not fill.

What 应届生 status means for you

Employers hiring through campus channels restrict applications to 应届毕业生, meaning students in their graduation year. That status is time-limited and generally does not survive a gap year, which is why applying during your final year matters more in China than in most countries. Once you graduate and leave that window, you are competing as an experienced hire instead.

Which fairs and employers accept international students

Campus job fairs, called 双选会, are open events where employers set up stands and students queue with printed CVs. Access rules vary: some universities open them to all registered students, others restrict entry to Chinese nationals because employers cannot sponsor work permits. The reliable move is to ask your school's employment centre for the list of employers that have previously hired foreign graduates.

State-owned enterprises and defence-linked employers usually require Chinese citizenship, and some roles are closed to foreign nationals by regulation. Private technology firms, foreign-invested companies and the overseas-facing divisions of large Chinese groups are far more open, particularly for roles involving your home market, a European language, or international sales and operations.

The internship route beats the fair route

An internship conversion is the single most reliable path from a Chinese degree to a Chinese job, because the employer already knows you and has already solved the sponsorship question internally. Students who start interning in the second half of year three usually have an offer before autumn recruitment ends, while students who only attend fairs are competing against hundreds of domestic candidates per role.

What Chinese recruiters expect from a CV

A Chinese graduate CV is one page, includes a photograph, and lists education, internships, projects, skills and languages in that order, with dates in reverse chronological order. Two pages is acceptable only for research or design roles with a portfolio. Chinese recruiters scan for school name, major, internship brand names and measurable outcomes, so lead with those.

Write the CV in Chinese for domestic employers and in English only for foreign-invested firms or overseas-facing roles. A Chinese-language CV that reads as machine-translated is worse than no Chinese CV, because it signals you cannot work in the language.

The forms your school issues

Chinese graduates receive an employment recommendation form and, historically, a tripartite agreement signed by student, employer and university. International students are often excluded from the tripartite system, so confirm with the employment centre what documentation you will be given. Employers who insist on the tripartite agreement are usually not set up to hire foreign nationals.

The work permit question, answered early

A foreign graduate needs an employer-sponsored work permit, which in most cities means a job offer, a degree certificate and a clean criminal record check, followed by a Z visa and a work-type residence permit. The usual two-year post-graduation experience requirement is waived for graduates of Chinese universities under a concession introduced in 2017, but the waiver is applied by the local science and technology bureau, not automatically.

Shanghai and Beijing's Zhongguancun pilot zones have gone further, allowing foreign graduates of local universities to work directly after graduation. Outside those zones, rules vary by city, and the hiring company's willingness to handle the paperwork matters more than your qualifications.

Ask this before the second interview

Ask directly whether the company has sponsored a foreign work permit before, and which city office handled it. A recruiter who cannot answer has probably never done it, and the process will stall after you accept. Companies with international teams usually have a standard answer within a day.

A twelve-month plan that works

Treat recruitment as a project with a fixed deadline rather than something you do in your final semester. Chinese campus recruitment closes on a schedule, and the autumn wave is effectively finished by December. The plan below starts in year three and assumes you want a job in China, not only a degree:

  1. Build a Chinese-language CV by the start of year three, and have a Chinese classmate read it aloud for errors.
  2. Apply for an internship related to your target function by month three of year three.
  3. Sit HSK 5 if your target employers interview in Chinese, because graduate interviews are conducted in Chinese at nearly all domestic firms.
  4. Attend autumn information sessions in September of your final year, and ask about sponsorship at the stand rather than by email.
  5. Track applications in a spreadsheet with deadlines, because the online systems close without reminders.
  6. Keep your internship manager as a reference and ask about conversion before the autumn wave ends.

The students who land jobs in China are rarely the ones with the best grades. They are the ones who started interning a year early, learned to write a Chinese CV that recruiters recognise, and asked the sponsorship question before signing anything.
