Campus Clinic and Hospital Referral in China

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-27

Healthcare in China is inexpensive by international standards but procedurally strict, and the campus clinic sits at the centre of the system for students. It is the first point of contact, the gatekeeper for insurance reimbursement and, for most everyday illness, the only place you need to go. Understanding the sequence saves both money and a wasted afternoon. This guide explains how the campus clinic china university arrangement works in practice.

The campus clinic is your first stop, by rule

Every Chinese university operates a clinic or campus hospital, and the insurance scheme attached to your student status requires you to attend it first for non-emergency care. Walking directly into a large public hospital is allowed, but without a referral letter the reimbursement rate drops sharply or the claim is refused. The clinic is usually open on weekdays from 8:00 to 17:30 with a night duty desk.

Where it is and when it is open

Most campuses place the clinic near the dormitory zone, and the location is printed on the campus map issued at registration. Weekday hours cover routine consultations; outside those hours a duty nurse handles emergencies and arranges transport.

What the clinic can and cannot treat

The clinic handles colds, fevers, stomach upsets, minor injuries, dressing changes, vaccinations and routine blood or urine tests, and it issues the sick notes that academic offices require. It cannot manage surgery, imaging beyond basic X-ray, or specialist care, and it will refer you upward for anything requiring a CT scan, an endoscopy or a specialist opinion.

The consultation itself

Registration takes a minute at the desk with your student card and passport; the consultation fee is typically 5 to 20 yuan. Appointments are not usually needed for general medicine. Bring a translator or use a translation app if your Chinese is limited, and write your symptoms down in advance, since consultations are short and frequently interrupted.

Referral letters and how they unlock reimbursement

When the clinic cannot treat you, it issues a referral letter naming a specific hospital, usually a tertiary A institution, and that letter is the document your insurance claim depends on. Keep the original, because the hospital will ask for it at registration and the insurer will ask for a copy. Referrals are usually valid for a single visit or a short window.

What the letter contains

The letter records your diagnosis, the reason for referral, the named hospital and the date, stamped with the clinic seal. Photograph it before handing it over. If the hospital keeps the original, ask for a stamped copy at the registration desk, because the reimbursement office will not accept a photograph on its own.

Student medical insurance in practice

Full-time students are enrolled in the urban and rural resident basic medical insurance scheme, arranged by the university and paid annually, with a student contribution of roughly 350 to 400 yuan a year and the rest subsidised. Enrolment happens in the autumn, and a student who misses the window may have no cover until the next cycle.

How claims are actually paid

At many universities the clinic settles directly and you pay only the co-payment. For hospital treatment you pay in full and claim afterwards at the insurance office, submitting the referral letter, the itemised invoice, the prescription and the discharge summary. Claims are processed monthly and reimbursement arrives by bank transfer, typically within four to eight weeks.

Going to a hospital without a referral

You may go directly to any hospital, and for urgent problems you should. What changes is the money: without a referral the reimbursement rate falls and part of the cost becomes yours. Public hospitals also run on appointment systems, so register through the hospital's WeChat account or app with your passport number rather than queueing at 6:00 for a same-day slot.

Registration, payment and the queue

Chinese public hospitals separate registration, consultation, testing, pharmacy and payment into different counters or floors. Expect to pay before each step, not once at the end. Keep every receipt in one envelope, since the insurance office requires the full set and a missing slip can void the claim for that visit.

Emergencies: when to call 120

Call 120 for an ambulance, 110 for police and 119 for fire, and note that these numbers work from any phone including a foreign SIM. For a fever, a sprain or food poisoning, go to the campus clinic or the nearest hospital emergency department yourself. For chest pain, breathing difficulty, heavy bleeding or loss of consciousness, call 120 and stay on the line.

What to say on the call

Give the campus name, the building and the nearest gate, then the patient's condition and age. English-speaking dispatchers are not guaranteed, so ask a Chinese-speaking classmate to call if you can, and have someone wait at the gate to guide the ambulance. Ambulance charges are modest but not free and are usually paid at the hospital.

Prescriptions, pharmacies and bringing medicine in

Prescription medicines require a doctor's prescription, and pharmacies will not dispense antibiotics or controlled drugs without one. Over-the-counter remedies for colds, pain and digestion are sold freely and are cheap. If you take regular medication, bring a supply with the original packaging and a doctor's letter, since some common foreign brands are unavailable or sold under different names.

Getting a repeat prescription

For ongoing conditions, register with a hospital specialist rather than relying on the clinic, and ask for a prescription covering three months where the drug allows it. Keep the Chinese generic name written down, because pharmacy staff recognise the chemical name more reliably than the brand you use at home.

Costs to expect, and how to keep receipts

A clinic consultation costs the price of a coffee; a specialist visit at a tertiary hospital runs 50 to 300 yuan depending on the department, and an overnight admission with tests can reach several thousand. The insurance exists to absorb exactly that tail. Photograph every invoice and keep the paper, because claims are rejected for missing documents far more often than for ineligible treatment.

The claim file should contain, at minimum:

  • the referral letter, with the clinic seal visible
  • the itemised invoice, not just the payment slip
  • the prescription or the pharmacy receipt
  • the discharge summary, where there was an admission
  • a copy of your passport page and student card

Treat the clinic as the front door rather than an obstacle, get the referral letter when it is offered, and file the paperwork the same week. That routine keeps ordinary illness cheap and, more importantly, keeps a serious episode from becoming a financial one in a system whose rules are predictable once you know the sequence.
