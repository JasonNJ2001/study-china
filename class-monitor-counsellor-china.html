Class Monitor, Counsellor and Student Affairs in China

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-28

Chinese universities run on a two-layer pastoral system that most international students never have explained to them. A student class monitor handles peer coordination, while a salaried counsellor holds the administrative authority that decides whether your leave is approved, your award application is forwarded and your discipline record stays clean. Understanding who does what turns a confusing bureaucracy into three named people you can actually reach.

What a class monitor actually does

A class monitor (班长, bānzhǎng) is an elected or appointed student who relays official notices, collects documents, tracks attendance and coordinates class activities for one cohort of roughly 25 to 40 students. The role carries no salary and no academic credit, but it gives international students a direct line to the counsellor who signs off on leave requests and award nominations. Most monitors are chosen in the first two weeks of the semester.

The monitor is not a decision-maker. They forward, remind and collect. When a notice says documents are due on Friday, the monitor is the person who tells you on Wednesday that you have two days left. In practice this makes them the single most useful contact in your first semester.

What a monitor is expected to relay

Attendance sheets, course selection windows, scholarship and award notices, holiday travel reporting, dormitory inspections and examination room assignments all pass through the class group. Monitors also collect signatures for class activities and pass on complaints that individual students are reluctant to raise directly.

Why the role matters more for international students

Notices are published in Chinese, on portals designed for domestic students, with deadlines that assume you already know the academic calendar. A monitor who translates and forwards deadlines compensates for most of that gap, which is why many international students stand for the post themselves.

The counsellor is your real gatekeeper

Every class is assigned a counsellor (辅导员, fǔdǎoyuán), a university employee who manages attendance, discipline, scholarships and dormitory issues for roughly 150 to 300 students across several classes. Counsellors keep office hours but usually answer WeChat messages faster, and they sign the forms that later reach the international students office. Learning their name, office number and WeChat ID in week one saves weeks of paperwork later.

The counsellor is a university employee, not a volunteer, and their performance is measured partly on attendance and retention in their classes. That gives them a strong incentive to keep you enrolled and compliant, and a matching incentive to notice when you disappear for two weeks.

The four documents that need a counsellor signature

Four documents pass through the counsellor before they become official: leave requests longer than three days, certificates of enrolment (在读证明), scholarship and award nominations, and disciplinary statements. Each requires your student number, the exact dates involved and, for leave, a reason the university can file. Missing dates are the most common reason a form comes back unsigned.

Leave requests and the three-day threshold

Short absences are handled inside the class. Anything over three consecutive days goes up to the counsellor, and anything over two weeks usually needs the school-level dean as well. Medical leave requires a hospital certificate, not a clinic note, once it passes one week. Leaving without approval is recorded as absenteeism, and enough of it triggers an academic warning.

Certificates of enrolment and official letters

The certificate of enrolment is the document banks, visa offices and scholarship bodies accept as proof of study. Request it from the counsellor at least three working days before you need it, since it is printed on university letterhead and stamped by a separate office. Ask for the bilingual version if you will use it abroad.

How notices reach you, and how they get missed

Official notices travel through at least three channels: the class WeChat group, the university portal, and printed boards outside the teaching building. Only the portal is authoritative, and only the portal timestamps publication. Students who rely on WeChat alone regularly miss course selection and scholarship deadlines, because group messages scroll out of view within hours of posting.

Check the portal twice a week even when nothing seems to be happening. Deadlines for award applications in particular are announced with as little as five days' notice, and late submissions are almost never accepted.

Scholarships, awards and the class vote

Most Chinese universities allocate internal scholarships through two steps: the counsellor ranks eligible students by grade point average and conduct score, then the class votes or the department committee confirms. Rankings are published for three to five days so students can challenge errors in their conduct score, which covers attendance, dormitory behaviour and participation, before the list is finalised.

The conduct score is the part international students underestimate. It can move a student up or down several places in a ranking where the top ten students may be separated by less than one grade point.

Running for class monitor

Elections are held in the first two weeks of the academic year and again each September. International students can stand, and many do, because the post closes the information gap in a single move and adds a line to any later scholarship or graduate application. Budget two to four hours a week for notices, forms and event logistics, more during examination and award season.

Before you accept the role, confirm that your Chinese is good enough for written notices and that your programme allows the time. A monitor who cannot read the notice they are forwarding creates more problems than they solve.

Practical habits that prevent most problems

  • Save the counsellor's office number, extension and WeChat on the first day, not the first emergency.
  • Screenshot every deadline you receive and put it in your own calendar with a two-day buffer.
  • Keep scanned copies of your passport, admission letter and insurance card in one folder on your phone.
  • Ask for documents in writing; verbal approvals are hard to prove later.
  • Read the conduct score rules once at the start of term so ranking season holds no surprises.

The class monitor and counsellor chain is unglamorous, but it is where attendance, awards and paperwork are actually decided. Students who learn it in the first month spend the rest of the year on coursework instead of chasing signatures.
