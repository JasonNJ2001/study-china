Studying in China Under 18: Guardianship and Boarding Rules

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-06

Study in China under 18 is legal, but it is not the same application as an adult degree. A minor needs a legally appointed adult resident in China, a school authorised to enrol foreign minors, and a JW202 form that states the guardian arrangement. Most universities refuse applicants who turn 18 after the enrolment date, so the practical route is a boarding school or an international school, not a bachelor programme.

Where the legal line actually falls

The cut-off is set by the school, not by the visa. Universities overwhelmingly require applicants to be 18 by the enrolment date in early September, while primary and secondary schools accept foreign minors under the 2017 Measures for the Administration of International Students in Schools, Ministry of Education Order No. 42, in force since 1 July 2017. That order obliges the receiving school to supervise the minor and to record a guardian.

Why a 17-year-old is usually deferred

A student who turns 18 in October cannot start a bachelor programme in September, because admission systems check age against the enrolment date rather than the calendar year. Universities will hold the offer for the next intake instead of making an exception. Language year programmes are stricter still, since they feed directly into degree registration the following September.

The guardian requirement in practice

A guardian must be a resident adult in China, and the appointment has to be documented before the visa application, not after arrival. Chinese embassies ask for a notarised guardianship certificate naming the adult, their relationship to the student, and their address in China. A parent on a short visit does not qualify, because the requirement is continuous physical presence, not legal custody.

Three acceptable guardian arrangements

  1. A parent or relative who holds a valid Chinese residence permit and lives in the same city as the school.
  2. A Chinese citizen appointed by the family and accepted in writing by the school, with the notarised certificate attached to the visa file.
  3. A school-appointed residential guardian, offered by some boarding schools for an annual fee of roughly RMB 10,000 to 30,000.

The third option is the most common for families with no contact in China, and it is the one admissions offices ask about first.

What the notary has to say

The certificate is issued by a notary public in the student's home country and then legalised, or by a Chinese notary office if the guardian is already in China. It must name the student's full passport spelling, the guardian's ID or passport number, and the scope of authority, which should cover medical consent, school correspondence and emergency decisions. Vague wording such as "in loco parentis" is routinely rejected.

Boarding schools versus university pathways

Boarding is the only realistic option before 18, because Chinese public schools do not enrol foreign minors living without a resident parent. International schools and the international divisions of public high schools accept foreign passport holders, teach in English or bilingual streams, and provide dormitories with wardens on site. Tuition is the main barrier.

The dormitory rules that apply to minors

Boarding schools impose curfews, usually 21:30 to 22:30, weekend sign-out rules requiring the guardian's written approval, and a ban on living off campus. Guardians are contacted for any absence longer than one night. Universities apply the same principle through a signed off-campus permission form countersigned by a parent.

Documents the visa file needs

An under-18 file carries three items an adult file does not: the notarised guardianship certificate, a parental consent letter signed by both parents and notarised, and the school's confirmation of residential supervision. Everything else follows the standard X1 route for stays longer than 180 days, including the Foreigner Physical Examination Form, which is valid for six months from the examination date.

  • Passport valid for at least six months beyond the intended stay
  • Admission letter and JW202 form stating the guardian's name and address
  • Notarised guardianship certificate and both parents' consent letters
  • Foreigner Physical Examination Form, completed within the last six months
  • School confirmation that dormitory supervision is provided
  • Proof of funds covering tuition plus living costs for the first year

When the JW202 names a guardian

The JW202 is generated by the school through the provincial education department, and for a minor the guardian field is not optional. If it is left blank or lists an address in another province, the visa officer will query it and the application stalls. Ask the admissions office to confirm the wording before the form is issued, because reissuing it means a new provincial filing.

Costs and the financial guarantee

Beyond tuition, a guardian arrangement, insurance and boarding fees push the first-year budget to roughly RMB 150,000 to 300,000 at an international school, against RMB 60,000 to 90,000 at a university. The visa officer looks for the full first-year figure in a bank account, held for at least 28 days in most consular districts.

What to do if the student turns 18 mid-programme

Nothing changes at 18 except the paperwork. The guardianship requirement lapses, the student may sign their own off-campus permission, and the residence permit continues without interruption. Schools usually ask for a short confirmation letter at that point, and the file is converted to adult status at the next permit renewal rather than immediately.

If the plan is a degree rather than a boarding place, the timing question matters more than the paperwork: delay the intake by one year so the student is 18 in September, and the whole guardianship layer disappears. Families who need a decision on routes, documents and timing should compare programmes and ask for a document check before filing.
