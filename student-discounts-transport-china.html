"Student Discounts, Transport Cards and Cheap Travel in China"

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-09

Living costs in China are low enough that most students stop optimising after the rent, but the discount layer underneath is real and almost nobody explains it to foreigners. A student card opens half-price museum and attraction tickets, campus gyms and software, while the famous railway student fare is closed to anyone whose home address is outside China.

Do international students get discounts in China?

Yes, on attractions, museums, campus facilities and software, wherever a valid student ID is accepted at the ticket window. The discount is normally 50% on the published adult ticket, applied at the counter rather than online, and it usually requires the physical card rather than a photograph of it. Carry the card; a screenshot persuades nobody.

No, on the railway student fare. The 50% hard-seat discount on 12306 is tied to a student discount card with a magnetic strip that encodes your home station, and it covers four round trips a year between the university city and that station. If your home is Lagos, Almaty or São Paulo, there is no eligible route, so the discount does not apply.

The attractions where a student card halves the price

State-owned museums have been free since 2008, so the big national collections in Beijing, Shanghai, Xi'an and Nanjing cost nothing at all. The savings come from paid heritage sites: the Forbidden City charges RMB 60 in peak season and RMB 40 in winter, the Terracotta Army RMB 120, and the Summer Palace RMB 30 in peak season, all half price with a student card.

Provincial parks, temples, cable cars and theme parks vary. Some apply the discount only to Chinese students and check the ID photo against the passport, so bring both. Private attractions such as aquariums and safari parks rarely discount at all, and online travel platforms sell the adult ticket by default, which is why the counter is often cheaper than the app.

What to say at the ticket window

Show the student card and the passport together and say "xuesheng piao" (student ticket). At sites with automated gates, buy at the manned window instead, because the machine reads only Chinese identity cards.

Where the discount does not exist

High-speed rail, domestic flights, hotels and ride-hailing have no student tier. Airlines occasionally run youth fares, but they are rare and usually worse than booking three weeks ahead on a low-cost carrier.

City transport: metro, bus and the transport card

Metro fares run RMB 3-10 per journey, charged by distance, and every large city sells a stored-value transit card at station service desks for a RMB 20-30 deposit. The card saves the queue at ticket machines and works on buses too, but it gives no discount in most cities, so its value is convenience rather than price.

Buses are the cheap option at RMB 2 flat in most cities, and they are also the slowest. For daily commuting, the arithmetic usually favours a bicycle: a shared-bike monthly pass costs RMB 15-25 and covers unlimited 30-minute rides, which beats RMB 6 a day on the metro for anyone living within three kilometres of campus.

Paying with your phone

WeChat and Alipay both carry a metro QR code that works in most cities without a transit card, but both require a Chinese bank account to top up. Set that up in the first week, because cash on a city bus is increasingly awkward and some routes no longer accept it.

Trains: how to book and where the money goes

Book on 12306, the official railway app, which accepts foreign passports after identity verification at a station counter. Tickets go on sale 15 days before departure, high-speed G trains run at 300-350 km/h, and the Beijing-Shanghai line takes 4.5 hours for about RMB 553 in second class.

The cheap seats are the ones students forget. A Z or T overnight sleeper costs less than half the high-speed fare for the same route and saves a hotel night, and hard sleeper berths are safe, reasonably clean and full of students. The catch is time: Shanghai to Chengdu is 12 hours by high-speed rail and 26 by sleeper.

Cheap travel that actually works

Travel in the shoulder seasons. The first week of October, the Spring Festival holiday and the May Day break triple hotel prices and fill every train, while the same route in late November or early March is quiet and cheap. University timetables make this easy: mid-term weeks are the cheapest travel windows of the year.

Book accommodation as a hostel dorm rather than a hotel room, at RMB 60-120 a night in most cities, and use the metro rather than taxis from the station. For food, campus canteens charge RMB 10-20 a meal, and the same dishes near a tourist site cost four times as much.

The four bookings to make early

  • Overnight sleepers for long routes, which sell out first
  • High-speed tickets for the first day of any public holiday
  • Hostel beds in Xi'an, Chengdu and Hangzhou during peak season
  • Airport buses, which are a fraction of a taxi and run until the last flight

Campus discounts most students never claim

The student card covers more than tickets. University gyms charge RMB 5-15 a visit against RMB 50-100 commercially, campus clinics offer consultations at nominal cost, and the library supplies databases that would cost a fortune at home. Software is the biggest single saving: Apple, Microsoft, Adobe and most statistical packages sell education licences at 40-70% off with a student email.

Cinema tickets drop to RMB 25-40 on weekday afternoons with a student card, and several chains run student Mondays. Language courses, test preparation and driving schools near campus advertise student rates openly, and asking "you hui ma?" (is there a discount?) at any independent shop costs nothing and occasionally works.

What the discounts add up to

A student who uses the card for six heritage sites, a gym membership, one software licence and a bike-share pass saves roughly RMB 1,200-2,000 over an academic year, which is one month of living costs in a second-tier city. The effort is a card you already have and a habit of asking at the counter instead of booking online.

The trap is assuming the discount is universal. Rail fares, flights and hotels are priced for everyone, and the student fare that Chinese classmates mention does not exist for you. Ask at the window, carry the card and the passport, and treat every saving as a bonus rather than a right.
