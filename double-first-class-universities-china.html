Double First-Class Universities in China: Does the Shuang Yiliu Label Change Your Application?

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-15

Chinese universities are marketed with a confusing set of labels: 985, 211, Double First-Class, provincial key. The newest of them drives national funding today and increasingly shapes how applicants build a shortlist. Knowing what the label decides, and what it leaves untouched, is worth twenty minutes before you pay an application fee.

What the Shuang Yiliu Label Actually Means

Double First-Class (双一流, shuang yiliu) is a rolling Ministry of Education list, launched in September 2017 and revised in February 2022, that names specific universities and specific disciplines for priority funding. The current edition covers 147 universities and 331 disciplines. Unlike earlier designations it is not permanent: entries are reviewed on a five-year cycle and can be added or removed.

The list has two layers that applicants routinely confuse. A university can be listed as a whole institution, or only for named disciplines, or both. A university listed only for, say, materials science is not a "Double First-Class university" in the everyday sense; it holds one funded discipline. This distinction decides whether the label says anything about the programme you actually want.

Why 985 and 211 still appear everywhere

The 211 project ran from 1995 and named 112 universities; the 985 project ran from 1998 and named 39. Both were closed to new entrants when Double First-Class replaced them as the funding framework, but the abbreviations survive in recruitment advertising, agent brochures and Chinese employers' screening habits. A university described as "985 and Double First-Class" is telling you it was elite before 2017 and still is.

How Double First-Class Status Affects Admission Decisions

For self-funded applicants the label works as a screening signal, not a rule. Listed universities receive more applications per available place, set higher subject thresholds, and close some English-taught programmes once quotas fill. No regulation requires a university to be listed, and a qualified applicant is never refused for choosing an unlisted institution.

What changes in practice is timing and selectivity. A listed university's international office can afford to wait for stronger files, so a complete application submitted in December is treated very differently from the same file in May. Unlisted provincial universities often review on a rolling basis and answer within two weeks.

Where the Label Matters Most: Money and Recognition

Funding follows the list, which is why the label has real consequences for scholarship applicants. Chinese Government Scholarship places, provincial scholarships and most university-level tuition waivers are concentrated in listed institutions, and a supervisor with a funded laboratory can support a student far more easily there than elsewhere.

Scholarship allocation

Chinese Government Scholarship quotas are distributed to designated host universities rather than to students directly, so the institution on your admission letter determines which scholarship pool you can be considered for. Applying to a strong programme at an unlisted university is not fatal, but the funding route narrows to provincial and self-funded options.

Degree recognition and graduate school

Recognition authorities abroad generally assess the institution and the programme, not the Chinese label. Employers in China, however, use it as a first filter: many state-owned enterprises and municipal recruitment schemes name 985, 211 or Double First-Class in their eligibility rules. If you intend to work in China after graduation, the label carries weight that it does not carry in Europe or North America.

Where the Label Matters Least: Teaching Inside a Programme

A funded discipline and a well-taught classroom are different things. Funding concentrates in research equipment and senior faculty, while the courses an undergraduate actually sits in are delivered by lecturers whose quality varies by school within the same university. A provincial normal university with a forty-year record in teaching Chinese as a foreign language can outperform a listed science university's language centre.

Class size and access are the practical tests. Ask how many students share a laboratory bench, whether undergraduates may join a supervisor's project before the final year, and how many of the programme's courses are taught in English after year two. Those answers predict your experience more reliably than any list.

How to Check Whether a Programme Is Listed

Check the discipline, not the university name. The February 2022 list is published by the Ministry of Education as two documents, one for institutions and one for disciplines, and a university's own admissions site usually reproduces the relevant lines on its international student page. If a recruiter's brochure claims the label without naming a discipline, treat it as marketing.

Reading a listing correctly

Match the exact discipline name to your intended major. "Engineering" is not a discipline on the list; "civil engineering", "chemical engineering" and "mechanical engineering" are separate entries, and a university may hold one of them and not the others. A programme that sits in a listed discipline usually shares the lab budget; a programme in an unlisted discipline at the same university does not.

Questions to put to the international office

Ask three things in writing: whether the specific programme is inside a listed discipline, how many international students the programme admitted last year, and which scholarship pools the programme can access. Answers in writing are useful later, because admission letters and scholarship decisions are issued by the same office.

Choosing Between a Labelled University and a Better Fit

Rank the criteria before you look at names, because the label is persuasive and will otherwise decide for you. Five checks resolve most of the confusion, and none of them needs an agent or a paid consultation: each takes under an hour and leaves you with something in writing, which matters when an offer arrives and the deadline is a week away.

  1. Confirm the discipline is on the current list, not just the university.
  2. Compare total cost for the full programme, including the city's living costs.
  3. Ask which years are taught in English and which switch to Chinese.
  4. Check whether the programme's graduates are eligible for the scholarship you need.
  5. Visit or video-call the campus and speak to a current student in the programme.

A listed university in a smaller city with a strong programme usually beats an unlisted one in a tier-one city with a weak programme. The reverse holds if you plan to work in China, where the label opens doors that a better-taught programme may not. Decide which future you are buying.

If you want a second opinion on how a specific programme sits against the current list, have an adviser review the admission letter and the scholarship route before you accept an offer.
