Your First Week in China: SIM Card, Bank Account and Police Registration

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-02

The first week in China is not about sightseeing. It is a sequence of registrations, and the sequence matters: several of them unlock the next one, and one of them has a 24-hour deadline that most arrival guides bury in a footnote.

This is the order that works, based on what actually blocks students in their first seven days.

Before you land: the two things that save a day

Two items belong in your hand luggage, not your checked bag. The first is your admission package: the admission letter and the JW202 or JW201 form, in original. The second is a set of passport photographs, at least eight, white background. Both are required repeatedly in week one, and both are painful to replace.

Also notify your university's international office of your flight number and arrival time. Most run a pickup service during the main intake window, and a pickup means you skip the taxi negotiation and, more importantly, arrive at a dormitory that knows you are coming.

Day 1: arrive, register your address, sleep

The 24-hour rule applies from the moment you check in. If you stay in a hotel, the hotel files your registration automatically — ask for the printout anyway. If you move into a university dormitory, the dormitory office registers you, but confirm it happened and keep the paper. If you are renting, you and your landlord go to the local police station together within 24 hours with the lease and the landlord's ID.

This document, the accommodation registration form, is the input to almost everything else, including the residence permit. Get it on day one and the rest of the week runs smoothly.

Day 2: campus enrolment and the student card

Enrolment is usually a single hall with a queue of desks. Bring the originals of your admission letter, passport, visa page, photographs and any payment receipts. You will leave with a student card, a campus network account and a schedule of orientation sessions.

The student card matters more than it looks. It is your library access, your canteen payment method, sometimes your dormitory key, and — critically — one of the documents banks ask for.

Day 3: SIM card, then the phone number that unlocks everything

A Chinese mobile number is the spine of daily life. It is required for banking, for delivery apps, for bike rentals, for university systems and for the verification codes that everything else depends on.

Choosing a plan that lasts the year

Buy the SIM in a physical store, not from a reseller at the airport. Real-name registration requires your passport and takes ten minutes. Choose a plan with generous domestic data rather than a tourist package, because a student plan will be cheaper over a year and can usually be changed later at the same store.

Day 4: the bank account

Open the account at a branch near campus, and go in the morning. Bring your passport, student card, admission letter and — depending on the city and the branch — your residence permit or the receipt showing it is being processed.

The three questions to ask at the counter

Ask three questions before you leave the counter: whether the account can receive international transfers, what the daily transaction limit is, and whether mobile banking is enabled on the account. Students who skip the third question discover at a tuition deadline that they can only transact at a counter during office hours.

While you are there, link the card to WeChat Pay and Alipay. Both accept foreign cards, but foreign-card transactions above a small threshold attract a fee, so a domestic account is meaningfully cheaper for everyday spending.

Day 5: the health check and the residence permit application

Most cities require a physical examination at a designated clinic before issuing a residence permit. Book it early in the week; results can take several days, and the clinic is busiest in the two weeks after intake.

With the health check submitted, file the residence permit application at the municipal exit-entry hall. Bring every document from the table above plus photocopies of each. The office holds your passport during processing, so do not plan international travel until you have it back.

Days 6 and 7: the things that actually make life work

The five tasks worth the weekend

  • Install the apps you will use daily: WeChat, Alipay, a map application, a food delivery app, and a translation tool that works offline.
  • Find the nearest supermarket, the campus clinic and a pharmacy, and note their closing times.
  • Save the international office's phone number and your country's embassy contact in your phone, not in a notebook.
  • Join the international students' group chat. It is where room swaps, second-hand furniture and warnings about closed offices circulate.
  • Walk your route to the teaching building once before the first class, so the first morning is not a navigation exercise.

What to skip in week one

Buy it in week three, not week one

Do not open a second bank account, do not sign a private apartment lease before seeing the dormitory, and do not buy a bicycle, a scooter or a full set of kitchen equipment. Every one of those decisions is easier in week three, once you know your campus, your schedule and which direction the wind blows in winter.

The one thing to remember

Week one is a chain, not a list. Police registration feeds the residence permit; the student card feeds the bank account; the phone number feeds the bank's verification. Do them in order and the week costs you four half-days. Do them out of order and it costs you two weeks of standing in the wrong queue.

If you are still at the application stage and want the arrival sequence planned against your intake date, that preparation is worth doing before the flight rather than after it.
