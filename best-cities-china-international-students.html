Best Cities in China for International Students

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-10-23

Most city rankings for China are written by people who visited for a week. They reward skylines and nightlife, then ignore the two things that decide whether your year abroad is comfortable: how much money leaves your account each month, and whether you can get real work experience before you graduate.

This guide picks the best cities for international students in China on three unglamorous criteria instead. Every figure below is a monthly living estimate excluding tuition, based on a shared or university dorm room rather than a private apartment in the city centre.

What actually decides whether a city works for you

  • Monthly burn rate. Rent, food and transport together. A city can cost 40 percent more per month without offering anything extra in your field.
  • Internship density. Whether companies in your sector are physically there. Finance is concentrated, tech is concentrated, and pretending otherwise wastes a year of networking.
  • Foreign student population. A large existing community means English-friendly clinics, dorm staff who have processed a hundred visa renewals, and restaurants that do not require pointing at pictures.

Tier one: highest opportunity, highest burn

Beijing

The university concentration is unmatched. If your field is policy, journalism, international organisations or academic research, Beijing is not a choice so much as a requirement, and the guest lectures and recruiting events at Tsinghua and Peking University do not travel elsewhere.

The cost of that access is distance. Haidian campuses sit far from the business districts, so a seventy-minute commute each way is normal for interns. Budget 4,500 RMB per month, more for a private room.

Shanghai

Shanghai is the most comfortable first city for someone arriving with no Mandarin. Signage and metro announcements are bilingual, and the foreign business community is dense enough that English-only internships genuinely exist.

It is also the most expensive. Expect 5,000 RMB per month as a realistic baseline, with private housing pushing toward 8,000. The return is that finance, consulting and consumer brand internships cluster within a few metro stops, so stacking two part-time roles is logistically possible.

Shenzhen

Shenzhen rewards anyone studying engineering, industrial design or supply chain. The hardware ecosystem lets a student move from a classroom project to a factory prototype in a weekend, and the pace of hiring is faster than anywhere else in the country.

Public transport is excellent and the young population skews the social scene toward working professionals. Costs sit near Shanghai, at 4,500 to 6,000 RMB per month.

The cities that beat tier one on value

Hangzhou

Hangzhou is the strongest value proposition for technology and business students. Alibaba's presence created a layer of platform, logistics and design companies that hire interns continuously, while living costs stay 25 to 30 percent below Shanghai.

Nanjing

Nanjing is underrated because it is quiet. Strong engineering and pharmaceutical universities, a well-preserved centre, and costs roughly 30 percent below Shanghai for a comparable standard of living. For a student who wants to focus rather than network relentlessly, that trade is worth taking.

Guangzhou

Guangzhou is the best city for anyone connected to trade or sourcing. The Canton Fair generates a recurring wave of short-term internships and translation work, and the long-established African, Middle Eastern and Southeast Asian communities make settling in easier than the numbers suggest.

Chengdu and Wuhan

Chengdu has become a real software city, not just a lifestyle destination: gaming studios and mobile developers have expanded steadily, and a student can live centrally on 3,000 RMB per month. Wuhan beats everything on cost, at 2,300 to 3,500 RMB across a student population of over a million, anchored by optics, automotive and medical research. Both are weaker on international hiring than the coast.

Xi'an

Xi'an suits aerospace, energy, history and archaeology, and it is the cheapest large city here for daily life. The foreign student community is smaller, so Mandarin improves faster, which is either an advantage or a shock depending on your expectations.

What internships actually require

An internship in China is not automatic once you are enrolled, and the paperwork catches people out every semester.

University consent comes first

Almost every university requires written approval from the international student office before you accept a paid or unpaid position. Some departments grant it readily, others restrict internships to the final year. Ask during orientation, not in month eight.

The residence permit annotation

Your X1 residence permit needs an annotation from the local exit-entry administration recording that part-time work or internship is permitted, tied to your university's consent letter. Without it, even an unpaid internship is a visa violation rather than a CV line. The process takes two to three weeks, so start early.

Realistic pay

Stipends in Shanghai and Shenzhen typically run 150 to 300 RMB per day in technology and finance, and 100 to 180 RMB in marketing, education and operations. Unpaid placements exist but are increasingly read as a red flag by later employers.

How to choose in one afternoon

  • Write down the sector you want, then list five companies you would most like to intern at. Check which city each is headquartered in.
  • Compare your monthly budget with the cost column above. If the gap is wider than 1,500 RMB, the expensive city is not viable without a scholarship.
  • Check whether your target campus is central or suburban. A campus ninety minutes from the business district removes most of the advantage you are paying for.
  • Confirm the international student office's internship policy before accepting an offer.

Mistakes that cost a semester

  • Choosing a city for its tourism reputation, then discovering the campus sits in a development zone with one noodle shop.
  • Assuming a large city means easy internships, when the relevant companies are in another district and your visa annotation is not in place.
  • Budgeting from a brochure that lists dorm rates from 2019 and assumes three canteen meals every day.
  • Ignoring climate. Wuhan and Nanjing summers, and Harbin winters, are not marketing exaggerations.

The right city is the one where your monthly costs leave room for a flight home and your target sector has offices you can reach by metro. Everything else is secondary, and most of it is fixable after arrival.
