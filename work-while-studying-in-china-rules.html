Can You Work While Studying in China? Rules and Limits

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-10-25

Two myths travel faster than any official handbook. The first says international students in China are forbidden from earning a single yuan. The second says the residence permit works like a student card with a salary attached. Neither survives contact with a visa officer.

The accurate answer is narrower than either: you can work, but only inside a channel that your university and the local exit-entry bureau have both authorised, and only in the form they have written onto your residence permit. Anything outside that channel is treated as illegal employment, whatever the intention behind it. What follows is what work while studying in China actually permits, where the boundaries sit, and what happens to students who cross them.

What the law actually says

Foreign students hold a residence permit marked for study, not a work permit. That distinction is the whole foundation. The permit grants you the right to study at a named institution; paid activity is an exception carved out of it, and exceptions require approval.

The regulation that matters dates from 2018 and covers two activities: part-time work and internships. Both are open to enrolled students, and both require the consent of your university plus registration or annotation by the public security exit-entry administration. The approval is specific, naming employer, location and often hours. It is not a general licence to take any job in any city.

In practice the university is the gatekeeper. Very few students walk into a police office alone and come out with an annotation. You apply through the international student office, and they decide whether the arrangement is defensible.

On-campus work comes first

Campus jobs are the path of least resistance, and the one most students can realistically get in their first year.

What is usually available

  • Library and reading-room assistant
  • Teaching or laboratory assistant, mostly for master's and doctoral students
  • International office support during admission season
  • Campus tour guide, translator or event helper
  • Research assistant funded by a supervisor's project

These roles are arranged by the university and paid through the university. Because the employer and the visa sponsor are the same institution, the annotation is usually a formality.

What it pays

Rates are modest and set locally. Expect roughly 15 to 40 RMB per hour for routine campus work, and more for translation or interpretation where a department has budget. A research assistantship paid from a grant may arrive as a monthly stipend of 800 to 2,000 RMB rather than an hourly wage.

Do not treat these numbers as income planning. They cover groceries and a phone bill, not tuition.

Off-campus part-time work is possible, with two approvals

This is where the corridor narrows.

Approval one: your university

The international student office has to agree that the job will not damage your attendance or your visa status. Many refuse outright for first-year undergraduates, and most require a minimum attendance or grade record. Bring a written offer naming the employer, address, duties and hours.

Approval two: the exit-entry bureau

With the university's letter, you apply at the local exit-entry administration for the annotation to be added to your residence permit. Some cities issue a separate note, some stamp the passport, and processing takes one to three weeks. Until that annotation exists you are not legal. A signed contract is not permission.

Hours in practice

There is no single national hourly cap, which is why forum advice contradicts itself. Local bureaus and universities set limits, commonly around 20 hours per week during term and more during official holidays. Ask your international office for the figure that applies in your city and get it in writing, because the officer who stops you will apply local practice, not a forum post.

Internships follow a separate track

A curricular internship, one required for your degree and arranged with the school's involvement, is normally recorded as part of your studies. That does not automatically make it paid, and if the company pays you, the payment still has to be defensible.

A non-curricular internship with a private company is riskier. Companies sometimes offer an unpaid placement with a small allowance, which is still compensation in the eyes of the bureau. Ask the school to confirm the arrangement in writing before you start, and keep the confirmation.

The clearest line: if the employer cannot appear on an official approval, the work is not legal. Cash tutoring feels harmless and is the most common violation.

Consequences

Under the exit-entry law, working without authorisation carries a fine of 5,000 to 20,000 RMB. Serious or repeated cases can add detention of five to fifteen days and deportation, and deportation normally carries a multi-year ban on re-entry.

The quieter cost is the record. A refusal or a violation note follows your file and resurfaces when you apply for a work permit or a longer residence permit after graduation. Students rarely weigh that against 100 RMB an hour.

How to do it properly

  1. Confirm your city's rules with the international student office, in writing.
  2. Secure a written offer naming employer, address, duties and hours.
  3. Submit the offer to the international student office for consent.
  4. Apply at the exit-entry bureau for the annotation and wait for it to be issued.
  5. Keep copies of the approval, the contract and your payslips.
  6. Re-apply if you change employer, city or working hours.

Is it worth the effort

For most students, campus work is. It is close, legal and flexible around exams. Off-campus part-time work pays off mainly when the job connects to your field, such as a lab, a design studio or a trading company, because the reference and the language practice matter more than the wage.

If money is the real problem, part-time work is a weak solution. A provincial scholarship, a cheaper city or a university with subsidised dormitory fees will move your budget far more than twenty hours a week at 30 RMB.

After graduation

Your student residence permit does not turn into work rights by itself. To stay and work you need an employer willing to sponsor a work permit, and you need to meet the qualifications. Graduates of Chinese universities have a route to a work permit without the usual two years of post-degree experience, but the employer still runs the process, and timing matters: start before your residence permit expires.

The students who handle this well are the ones who treat work rights as paperwork to be finished early, not as a risk to be tested.
