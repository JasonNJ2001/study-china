"Internships in China for International Students: Rules, Pay and Timing"

By ORIGIN Education · 2026-11-07

An internship in China can turn a degree into a job offer, or into a fine and a deportation order, and the difference is a stamp most students have never heard of. Chinese rules do allow foreign students to work part-time and to intern, but only through a formal approval chain involving the university and the local exit-entry bureau. Understanding that chain before you accept an offer is the whole game.

Can international students legally intern in China?

Yes, if three conditions are met: you hold an X1 visa converted to a study residence permit, your university approves the placement in writing, and the exit-entry administration annotates your residence permit for internship or part-time work. Internships arranged without that annotation are treated as illegal employment, regardless of whether you are paid, because Chinese law defines work by activity rather than by salary.

The rule was formalised in 2017, when the education, public security and human resources ministries issued guidance permitting foreign students to intern with institutional consent. Before that, any student work was unlawful; the change created a legal route, not a general right to work.

The annotation on your residence permit

The annotation (加注) is printed on the residence permit itself and states the permitted activity, the employer and often the hours. It is issued by the exit-entry administration of the public security bureau in the city where you study, on the basis of a university letter and the company's internship agreement. Processing takes seven to fifteen working days, and the permit is reissued rather than stamped.

The annotation is city-specific and employer-specific: annotated for a company in Nanjing, you are not covered for a Shanghai firm. Changing employer means a fresh application, so ask whether one annotation can name two placements.

What the university has to sign

The university's international student office signs a tripartite internship agreement (实习协议) between the student, the university and the company, and issues the supporting letter for the annotation. Some universities refuse placements that conflict with teaching hours, and a few refuse off-campus internships entirely in the first year. Ask the office which companies are already on their approved list before you apply anywhere.

What the company has to provide

The employer must hold a valid business licence, sign the tripartite agreement, and confirm the internship dates, the supervisor and the tasks. Companies that have never hired a foreign intern often do not know this, and a human resources department that says "just come in" is a warning rather than an opportunity. Offering to send them the university's template solves the problem in most cases.

What an internship actually pays

Intern pay in China is set by the market, not by the minimum wage, and it is low by Western standards. A Chinese company pays RMB 100 to 200 a day, an international firm RMB 150 to 250, and finance roles up to RMB 300. The legal floor is the hourly minimum wage: RMB 24 in Shanghai, RMB 26.4 in Beijing.

Timing: when to apply

The hiring calendar is compressed into two windows. Large companies open summer internship applications between March and May for a July start, and autumn recruitment for final-year students runs from September to November, when graduate schemes hire. Applying in June for a July start means competing for what is left, which is usually nothing at the good firms.

Summer versus term-time

A summer internship of eight to ten weeks fits inside the vacation and needs no adjustment to your course load, which is why it is the standard route. Term-time internships are legal but must not conflict with attendance, and universities monitor this because attendance records feed into visa renewal. Two days a week during term is realistic; four is not.

Where to find internships

The channels that work are narrower than the ones that advertise. University career centres place a small number of students each year but their partner companies are pre-vetted, which removes the annotation problem. Chinese platforms such as Shixiseng carry volume but most listings assume fluent Chinese. International firms recruit through their own campus programmes, which are in English and open early.

  • Start with the university's career centre and its list of approved employers
  • Ask the international office which companies have hosted foreign interns before
  • Apply to multinational campus programmes in March for a summer start
  • Use alumni networks: a referral skips the language screening
  • Check that the company can sign the tripartite agreement before interviewing

What a good listing looks like

A legitimate listing names the supervisor, the hours, the daily rate and the location, and it survives a search on the national enterprise credit system. Listings promising high pay for remote social media work, or unable to name a registered company, are the ones that lead to trouble.

The gap between internship and job

China has no post-study work visa. A graduate who wants to stay needs an employer to sponsor a Z work permit, which normally requires a bachelor's degree plus two years of relevant experience for a B-class permit. Fresh graduates rarely meet the experience test, which is why the internship matters: it is the evidence that converts an exception into an application.

Some free trade zones waive the two-year requirement for high-performing graduates of Chinese universities, and Shanghai's scheme is the best known. These are discretionary and employer-led, so intern at the company you want to join and raise the work permit question in your final month.

Mistakes that end in a fine

Working without an annotation is the expensive mistake. Chinese law treats unapproved employment as illegal work, with fines of RMB 5,000 to 20,000, possible detention and deportation. The employer is fined too, which is why legitimate companies ask for the annotated permit before your first day.

  • Accepting payment for freelance work, tutoring or online gigs on a student permit
  • Interns for a company while annotated for a different employer
  • Starting before the annotation is issued because the company was in a hurry
  • Assuming an X2 visa holder can intern: an X2 cannot convert to a residence permit
  • Skipping the university agreement because the company supplied its own contract

Students weighing a Chinese degree against the work experience it produces can compare programme lengths and city job markets before applying, because the internship route rewards those who start planning in the first year rather than the last.
